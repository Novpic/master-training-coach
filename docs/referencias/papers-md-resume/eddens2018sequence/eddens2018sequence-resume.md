# Summary — eddens2018sequence

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis tested whether the order of resistance and endurance exercise within a concurrent-training session changes training adaptations. Across the included programs, performing resistance exercise before endurance exercise improved lower-body dynamic strength more than the reverse order. The review found no clear sequencing effect for static strength, hypertrophy, maximal aerobic capacity, or body-fat percentage, and too little evidence to analyze power.

## Context and scope

The review included 10 studies (20 training groups; 245 participants overall, mean age 31 ± 16 years) in healthy adults older than 16 years. Six studies involved untrained participants, three recreationally active cohorts, and one professional-athlete cohort. Programs lasted at least 5 weeks, with at least 2 concurrent sessions per week; exercise modes were separated by no more than 15 minutes. Sequence was resistance-then-endurance versus endurance-then-resistance. Participant totals differed by outcome (227 for dynamic strength, 155 static strength, 137 hypertrophy, 184 aerobic capacity, and 167 body-fat percentage). Seven studies were rated strong and three moderate under the authors’ QualSyst assessment. The review covers heterogeneous program designs and fitness levels; most evidence does not represent a narrowly defined athletic group.

## Relevant findings

- **Dynamic lower-body strength:** Resistance followed by endurance produced a 6.91 percentage-point greater change than endurance followed by resistance (95% CI 1.96 to 11.87; p = 0.006). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 66%), so the pooled estimate should not be assumed to apply equally to every exercise selection or population.
- **Other outcomes:** The pooled sequence differences were not significant for lower-body hypertrophy (1.15 percentage points; 95% CI −1.56 to 3.87; p = 0.40; I² = 72%), static strength (−0.04 percentage points; 95% CI −3.19 to 3.11; p = 0.98), maximal aerobic capacity (−0.27 percentage points; 95% CI −2.74 to 2.20; p = 0.83), or body-fat percentage (0.68 percentage points; 95% CI −0.97 to 2.33; p = 0.42). These results indicate no demonstrated order advantage for those outcomes, not proof that all sequences are equivalent. There were too few studies to compare lower-body power.
- **Authors’ practical interpretation:** When a person must combine both modes in one session with little separation and dynamic lower-body strength is a priority, the authors favor resistance before endurance. The review does not establish this as a universal rule for people who can separate sessions, for outcomes other than dynamic strength, or for every concurrent-training program.
- **Limitations:** Only 10 studies qualified; effects varied across methods, age, training status, and outcome measurement. The search was limited to English-language sources, and publication bias could not be ruled out. Mechanisms such as residual fatigue were discussed as possible explanations, not directly established by the pooled analysis.
