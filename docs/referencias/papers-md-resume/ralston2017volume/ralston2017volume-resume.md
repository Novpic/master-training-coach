# Summary — ralston2017volume

## Central idea

This meta-analysis found small average strength advantages for some higher-versus-lower weekly set-volume comparisons per exercise. It supports a possible graded dose–response, but the evidence comes from few, heterogeneous male studies and does not establish an optimal set prescription for all trainees.

## Context and scope

The review searched five databases through September 2016 and included nine studies with 223 adult men (mean age 23.4 ± 2.18 years; four studies classified participants as trained and five as untrained). Eight studies used randomly assigned conditions without a control group; one was an RCT. Programs lasted 8–26 weeks, trained 2–4 days/week, and used approximately 73.5–85% of 1RM (reported mean 78.2 ± 4.1%); studies used 1–12 sets and 3–18 repetitions. Outcomes were pre-to-post 1RM strength for multi-joint and isolation exercises. The findings do not establish the same effects in women, older adults, or other populations.

## Relevant findings

- Weekly volume categories were **low (LWS) ≤5**, **medium (MWS) 5–9**, and **high (HWS) ≥10 sets per exercise per week**. As printed, the categories overlap at five sets, so classification of exactly five weekly sets is ambiguous.
- For pooled multi-joint and isolation outcomes, HWS versus LWS produced a small advantage (ES 0.18, 95% CI 0.06–0.30; p = 0.003). MWS versus LWS also showed a small advantage (ES 0.15, 95% CI 0.01–0.30; p = 0.04). For multi-joint exercises alone, HWS versus LWS was ES 0.18 (95% CI 0.01–0.34; p = 0.04). The combined exercise-specific 1RM comparison of HWS versus LWS was not statistically significant (ES 0.14, 95% CI −0.01 to 0.29; p = 0.06).
- **Isolation-exercise result is internally inconsistent:** the abstract and Results prose report HWS versus LWS (ES 0.23, 95% CI 0.06–0.40; p = 0.008), while the Figure 8 caption labels the comparison LWS versus MWS. The paper does not reconcile these descriptions, so this result should not be taken as reliable evidence distinguishing HWS from MWS for isolation exercises.
- **Author interpretation:** the authors cautiously favor MWS over LWS for beginners and novice trainees; for trainees between novice and advanced, they regard MWS or HWS as reasonable; for well-trained men, either MWS or HWS may be appropriate. They acknowledge that training-stage differences were not tested in well-controlled studies and that direct MWS-versus-HWS evidence was sparse. These are context-limited interpretations, not universal prescriptions.
- Effects were generally small. Reid’s data were influential, and removing them reduced the estimated volume effect. Other limitations include the small evidence base, mostly non-randomized pre/post designs, possible 1RM test-learning and exercise-specificity effects, protocol heterogeneity, and limited statistical power.
