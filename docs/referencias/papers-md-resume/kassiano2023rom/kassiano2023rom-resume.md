# Summary — kassiano2023rom

## Central idea

This systematic review examined how full versus partial resistance-exercise range of motion (ROM), including partial training at different portions of the movement, relates to muscle hypertrophy. Results suggest that training at longer muscle lengths—through full ROM or partial ROM in the initial portion—often produced greater growth than partial ROM limited to shorter muscle lengths, but effects varied by muscle and measurement site and the evidence base is small.

## Context and scope

The review included 11 longitudinal randomized trials (297 adults, 18 years or older, with or without resistance-training experience) lasting 5–15 weeks (mean 10 weeks). Trials compared full ROM with partial ROM or compared partial ROM at different parts of the excursion. Outcomes were site-specific muscle thickness, cross-sectional area, or volume; eight studies focused on lower-limb exercises and three on upper-limb exercises. Five studies included men only, one women only, and five mixed-sex samples. The authors calculated study-level percentage changes and effect sizes rather than presenting a pooled meta-analysis. The findings mainly concern healthy adults and the muscles studied; they do not establish a single optimal ROM for all exercises or muscle groups.

## Relevant findings

- **Review findings—quadriceps:** Full ROM and initial-partial ROM generally produced more favorable rectus femoris and vastus lateralis hypertrophy than final-partial ROM, particularly at distal measurement sites; reported between-group effect sizes across these comparisons were approximately 0.20–0.90. In one trial, initial partial ROM at longer lengths produced more proximal rectus femoris growth than full ROM (effect size 0.35–0.38). Other quadriceps trials found similar growth between conditions, including an isokinetic knee-extension comparison and an explosive leg-press study, so the pattern is not uniform.
- **Review findings—other muscles:** Full-ROM squats produced greater gluteus maximus and adductor growth than final-partial squats in one study (between-group effect sizes 0.24–0.25). Initial-partial preacher curls produced greater distal elbow-flexor thickness than final-partial curls in one trial, with similar proximal and middle-site changes. A single study in resistance-trained men found greater triceps brachii growth with middle-partial lying elbow extensions than with full ROM (between-group effect size 1.21); this result is preliminary rather than a general prescription.
- **Authors’ practical interpretation:** Retain full ROM as a broadly supported option for hypertrophy-oriented training, while considering selective initial-partial work in combination with full ROM where longer muscle lengths are reached. The review found less support for training only the final, shorter-length portion, but emphasized that the evidence is limited and muscle-specific. ROM recommendations should also account for exercise mechanics and the portion of the movement where resistance torque is greatest.
- **Limitations:** Evidence was concentrated in the quadriceps, with few studies of other muscles; samples and programs were heterogeneous, and six studies did not specify how ROM was measured. Several conclusions rely on one study, regional growth is not uniform, and the authors describe the proposed muscle-length mechanisms as plausible rather than established.

## Key formulas

The review calculated percentage change from pre- to post-training as:

$$
\Delta\% = \left(\frac{\text{post-training mean}}{\text{pre-training mean}}\times 100\right)-100
$$

Here, the pre- and post-training means are the group means for the muscle-size outcome; the result is the percentage change from baseline.

It calculated each training group’s standardized effect size as:

$$
ES = \frac{\text{post-training mean}-\text{pre-training mean}}{\text{pooled pre-training standard deviation}}
$$

The numerator is the change in the measured muscle-size outcome, and the denominator is the pooled baseline standard deviation for that outcome. The review used these study-level effects to compare ROM conditions; they are not pooled meta-analytic estimates.
