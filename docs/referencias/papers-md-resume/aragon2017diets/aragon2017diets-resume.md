# Summary — aragon2017diets

## Central idea

This International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand critically reviews how major dietary patterns and eating styles relate to body composition. Its central message is that sustained energy balance, sufficient protein and training context matter more than choosing a single named diet: several macronutrient patterns can produce similar body-composition outcomes when energy intake and adherence are considered.

## Context and scope

This is a society position stand based on a critical literature analysis, not a new intervention or a systematic review with pooled estimates. It focuses mainly on prospective interventions lasting at least 4 weeks that measured body composition, across hypocaloric, eucaloric and hypercaloric conditions, with and without exercise training. Studies of disease treatment were outside scope. The paper reviews energy-restricted, low-fat, low-carbohydrate/ketogenic, high-protein and intermittent-fasting approaches, as well as body-composition assessment methods. The authors note limited evidence in women and older adults and sparse research on meal frequency, macronutrient distribution and lifestyle strategies.

## Relevant findings

- **Author position—fat loss:** A sustained energy deficit is the primary mechanism for fat loss. The stand states that a larger deficit may be appropriate at higher starting body-fat levels, whereas slower loss may better preserve lean mass in leaner people. It cites a weight-loss example in athletes in which 0.7% of body weight per week preserved lean mass better than 1.4%; the 0.5–1.0% weekly rate is attributed to contest-preparation guidance, not established as a universal target.
- **Author position—lean-mass gain:** A sustained energy surplus is likely to better support lean-mass gain and rising resistance-training demands. The composition and size of the surplus, exercise, training status and individual response affect the balance of lean- and fat-mass change.
- **Diet composition:** A broad range from low-fat to low-carbohydrate/ketogenic patterns can be similarly effective for body composition. In controlled isocaloric, protein-matched feeding comparisons reviewed by the authors, lower-carbohydrate diets did not show a practically meaningful fat-loss or thermic advantage. Some low-carbohydrate meta-analytic findings in overweight/obese participants were statistically significant but small (about 0.57–1.46 kg), and could reflect differences in protein intake. Carbohydrate restriction may impair high-intensity or endurance performance; body-composition results should not be treated as performance guidance.
- **Protein:** Higher protein may support body composition, particularly with resistance training and energy restriction. The authors cite **2.3–3.1 g/kg fat-free mass (FFM) per day** as a possible range to maximize muscle retention in lean, resistance-trained people during hypocaloric conditions. Evidence for very high intakes above **3 g/kg** is described as emerging, not definitive; the denominator is not specified for this general statement.
- **Intermittent restriction:** The collective evidence did not show a general body-composition advantage over daily restriction. Individual trials and adherence differed, so this does not establish that every intermittent schedule is equivalent for every person or athletic goal.
- **Measurement and limitations:** No body-composition method is best for all settings; hydration, glycogen, measurement assumptions, availability, cost and operator skill affect interpretation. The position stand is evidence synthesis and recommendations, with many cited findings drawn from populations unlike healthy recreational adults.
