# Summary — schoenfeld2019frequency

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis tested whether training a muscle more often each week increases hypertrophy independently of weekly volume. Across volume-equated studies, higher and lower frequencies did not produce meaningfully different average muscle growth. When volume was not equated, higher frequency showed a modest association with greater hypertrophy, plausibly because it can help accumulate more work; this result does not establish an independent causal effect of frequency.

## Context and scope

The review searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and SPORTDiscus through August 2018 and included **25 randomized experimental studies** of adults without chronic disease or injury. Eligible comparisons trained muscle groups at different weekly frequencies, used traditional dynamic resistance exercise, assessed hypertrophy or lean body mass, lasted at least 6 weeks, and did not add another structured exercise program. Studies varied in training status, age, exercise selection, volume, measurement method, and whether sets were taken to failure. The primary pooled analysis focused on comparisons with training volume equated between groups.

## Relevant findings

- **Review finding—volume-equated training:** Across 13 studies (29 outcomes), higher versus lower frequency did not significantly affect hypertrophy (**effect-size difference 0.07; 95% CI −0.02 to 0.17; p = 0.11**). The estimated percentage-point difference was **1.2** (95% CI −0.33 to 2.7), and heterogeneity was low. No significant frequency effect was found in the direct-measurement, upper-body, lower-body, or trained-participant subgroups. Only three studies were available to assess volume-equated frequency in untrained participants, which was insufficient for that subgroup analysis.
- **Review finding—direct measurements and frequency ranges:** In volume-equated studies using direct muscle-size measures, the higher-versus-lower frequency difference was not significant for upper body (**p = 0.95**) or lower body (**p = 0.08**); the latter estimate was small and its confidence interval included no effect. The frequency-category meta-regressions likewise found no significant effect for 2, 3, or 4–6 days per week. These results do not establish that every schedule is interchangeable for every muscle or exercise.
- **Review finding—volume not equated:** Meta-regression detected an overall frequency effect when weekly volume differed between conditions (**p = 0.04**), favoring higher frequency. However, the estimated difference between 1 and 3 or more training days per week was modest (**effect size 0.18**), and confidence intervals for each frequency category included the null. Most studies in this subgroup involved untrained older adults, limiting transfer to younger or trained populations. Frequency may therefore be a way to distribute or accumulate volume, rather than a hypertrophy stimulus independent of volume.
- **Authors' practical interpretation:** For a given weekly volume, individuals can choose a muscle-training frequency based on preference and program feasibility. Higher frequency may help distribute work or increase volume, but the average benefit seen when volumes differed was modest. The paper notes that responses vary among individuals, so this pooled average should not be treated as a fixed prescription.
- **Limitations:** Direct measures were concentrated in upper arms and thighs, so other muscle groups may respond differently. There were too few studies to analyze untrained participants under volume-equated conditions or to fully separate age effects. Training factors such as tempo, rest, failure, and protein intake were not controlled uniformly; exercise type could not be isolated. Meta-analysis also cannot eliminate differences in study quality and protocol.
