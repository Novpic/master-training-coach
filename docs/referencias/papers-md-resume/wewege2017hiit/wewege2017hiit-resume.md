# Summary — wewege2017hiit

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis compared high-intensity interval training (HIIT) with moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) for body-composition outcomes in apparently healthy overweight or obese adults. Both approaches were associated with modest reductions in body-fat mass and waist circumference, with no detected between-program difference; HIIT required less weekly training time in the included trials. The apparent running-versus-cycling difference is a subgroup finding with limited evidence and should not be treated as a proven modality ranking.

## Context and scope

The review pooled 13 randomized or matched controlled trials involving 424 adults whose mean ages were 18–45 years and who did not have other diagnosed comorbidities such as diabetes or coronary artery disease. Programs lasted at least 4 weeks; average duration was 10.4 ± 3.1 weeks (range 5–16). HIIT used intervals up to 4 minutes at >85% of maximal heart rate (or a stated equivalent such as 80% maximal aerobic capacity or RPE 17); MICT used continuous exercise at 60–75% of maximal heart rate (or 50–65% maximal aerobic capacity or RPE 12–15). The review excluded programs combining the aerobic intervention with other exercise modes, though both groups could receive the same nutrition intervention.

## Relevant findings

- **Pooled within-group findings:** Both HIIT and MICT were associated with decreases in whole-body fat mass (about 1.7 kg with HIIT and 2.1 kg with MICT) and waist circumference (about 3 cm in each group). Neither intervention showed a statistically significant average change in body mass, lean mass, or trunk-fat measures. The authors cautioned that the modest fat-mass and waist changes may lie within measurement error when DXA/BIA or waist measurements are not tightly standardized.
- **Between-group comparison:** The review found no statistically significant HIIT–MICT difference for any body-composition outcome. These findings do not show that one mode is superior for fat loss.
- **Training time:** HIIT participants averaged 3.3 ± 0.7 sessions and 95 ± 46 minutes per week; MICT participants averaged 3.7 ± 0.9 sessions and 158 ± 46 minutes per week. The time difference favored HIIT (approximately 40% less weekly exercise time in these trials), not a claim that all HIIT prescriptions require less time.
- In the modality subgroup, treadmill-running interventions were associated with larger within-group fat-mass reductions (approximately 2.6 kg for each training mode), whereas cycling subgroups did not show significant changes on these measures. The physiological explanation was unclear, and the subgroup cannot establish that running itself caused the difference. Evidence for direct visceral-fat change was especially sparse; only one included study used CT to assess it.
- **Limitations and interpretation:** The pooled sample was small and follow-up short; study quality was moderate (mean modified PEDro score 4.6 ± 1.3), with high dropout, little assessor blinding, and incomplete adverse-event reporting. Running trials reported adverse events in 17 of 129 participants across four studies, while cycling safety data were too sparse for comparison. These results apply to the specified overweight/obese adult samples, not automatically to people with obesity-related comorbidities or to other populations.
- **Author conclusion:** HIIT may be a time-efficient option alongside MICT for short-term body-composition goals in the studied population. The review does not establish a universal protocol, long-term weight-management effectiveness, or comparative safety.
