# Summary — kerksick2017timing

## Central idea

This International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) position stand reviews carbohydrate and protein timing in healthy exercising adults, especially highly trained people. It places adequate total energy and daily macronutrient intake ahead of precise timing in most situations; timing is most consequential when exercise depletes glycogen and rapid recovery is needed, or when protein distribution helps meet daily needs.

## Context and scope

This is a narrative evidence review and professional-society position stand, not a single trial or a systematic meta-analysis. It covers acute studies and longer training interventions concerning carbohydrate, protein, meal timing/frequency, and pre-sleep protein. The authors note that much of the evidence comes from small studies, often involving men, and that direct tests of nutrient timing are relatively scarce. Recommendations are the ISSN panel’s position, not a single universally tested protocol; applicability depends on training volume/intensity, prior intake, recovery time, sex, and individual tolerance.

## Relevant findings

- **ISSN position—carbohydrate availability:** For high-performance athletes, carbohydrate intake is commonly reported as 5–12 g/kg/day; the position stand identifies 8–10 g/kg/day for athletes training at ≥70% VO₂max for about 12 h/week or more, and 8–12 g/kg/day in its practical guidance for athletes completing at least 8 h/week when rapid glycogen restoration is repeatedly needed. Carbohydrate loading is most relevant before prolonged, glycogen-depleting events; much of this evidence is from endurance athletes, particularly men.
- **ISSN position—before and during exercise:** For longer, higher-intensity exercise (>90 min and ≥70% VO₂max), the review commonly recommends 1–4 g/kg carbohydrate in the hours before exercise. For bouts longer than 60 min at >70% VO₂max, its position statement gives approximately 30–60 g carbohydrate/hour from a 6–8% carbohydrate-electrolyte drink, with greater relevance to bouts lasting beyond about 70 min. Serving-volume details vary across the abstract and practical guidance (including different fluid-ounce ranges and intervals); the more consistent target is the hourly carbohydrate rate and solution concentration. Gastrointestinal tolerance and the exercise context matter.
- **ISSN position—rapid glycogen recovery:** When recovery time is under 4 h, the statement lists either carbohydrate at 1.2 g/kg/h (favoring glycemic index >70), or carbohydrate at 0.8 g/kg/h combined with protein at 0.2–0.4 g/kg/h; it also lists caffeine at 3–8 mg/kg as a possible strategy. Elsewhere, the review concludes that adding protein tends to improve glycogen recovery when carbohydrate delivery is below 1.2 g/kg/h, with little added glycogen benefit when adequate carbohydrate is already supplied. These are conditional recovery strategies, not requirements after every workout.
- **ISSN position—protein:** Prioritize total daily protein (the practical guidance gives 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day). A high-quality protein dose of 20–40 g, approximately 0.25–0.40 g/kg per dose, every 3–4 h is presented as a pattern that supports muscle protein synthesis (MPS); approximately 10–12 g essential amino acids (EAA) can maximally stimulate MPS in the reviewed evidence. Protein before or soon after exercise can be practical, but the review reports that timing differences are often small when daily intake is adequate and a pre-exercise meal may reduce the importance of immediate post-exercise feeding.
- **ISSN position—pre-sleep and meal frequency:** Approximately 30–40 g casein before sleep can acutely increase overnight MPS. Longer-term results are less definitive: one 12-week study found improved strength and muscle mass but provided more total protein to the pre-sleep group, while another protein-matched comparison found no significant morning-versus-evening difference. Changing meal frequency alone has limited effects on weight loss or body composition in non-exercising populations; effects alongside exercise remain preliminary, though meal regularity may affect appetite and satiety.
- **Limitations and interpretation:** Acute MPS or glycogen responses do not necessarily establish long-term performance or body-composition effects. Most reviewed timing studies were small and male-dominated, and the article notes industry relationships among several authors. Treat the numerical guidance as the position stand’s context-dependent recommendations, not proof that exact timing is superior when total energy and nutrient intake are already sufficient.
