# Summary — morton2018protein

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis found that protein supplementation modestly augmented some resistance-exercise-training (RET) gains in healthy adults, with larger reported fat-free-mass (FFM) benefits in younger and resistance-trained participants. Its estimated daily-protein breakpoint is uncertain and should not be treated as a hard physiological ceiling or a universal prescription.

## Context and scope

The authors included 49 randomized controlled trials (1,863 healthy, non-energy-restricted adults) identified by searches through January 2017. Eligible RET lasted at least 6 weeks and occurred at least twice weekly; controls performed the same RET with placebo or no supplement. Programs varied widely: 6–52 weeks, 2–5 sessions/week, 1–12 sets/exercise, and 3–25 repetitions/set. The sample averaged 35 ± 20 years; only 10 studies included resistance-trained participants, and relatively few groups were exclusively female. Random-effects meta-analyses and study-level meta-regressions do not describe one uniform training or supplementation protocol.

## Relevant findings

- **Pooled findings:** compared with RET control, protein supplementation increased one-repetition-maximum (1RM) strength by **2.49 kg** (95% CI 0.64–4.33) and FFM by **0.30 kg** (95% CI 0.09–0.52). It also reduced fat mass by **0.41 kg** (95% CI −0.70 to −0.13) and increased muscle-fibre cross-sectional area by **310 μm²** (95% CI 51–570) and mid-femur muscle cross-sectional area by **7.2 mm²** (95% CI 0.20–14.30). Maximum voluntary contraction did not improve significantly (standardized mean difference 0.04, 95% CI −0.09 to 0.16). The fibre-area estimate was sensitive to inclusion of a high-risk study and warrants particular caution.
- Meta-regression suggested a larger FFM benefit in resistance-trained participants (coefficient 0.75 kg, 95% CI 0.09–1.40) and a smaller benefit with increasing age (−0.01 kg/year, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.00). These are study-level associations, not proof that training status or age caused different individual responses. Evidence in older adults was limited, and supplemental doses in those studies were often low.
- A two-segment analysis of 42 study arms (723 participants) estimated an FFM breakpoint at **1.62 g protein/kg body mass/day** (95% CI 1.03–2.20), but the segmental model was not statistically significant (p = 0.079). The authors interpreted approximately **1.6 g/kg/day** as sufficient for most RET-related gains and noted that the upper confidence limit, about **2.2 g/kg/day**, could be a pragmatic estimate for those seeking to maximize FFM gains. This is an uncertain pooled estimate, not a demonstrated sharp cutoff; evidence above the estimated breakpoint was limited.
- The authors emphasize that RET itself was a much stronger stimulus for strength and muscle adaptation than protein supplementation. These findings apply to the studied healthy, non-energy-restricted adults and should not be transferred directly to energy restriction, clinical populations, or every age and training group.
