# Summary — schoenfeld2017load

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis compared low- and high-load resistance training for strength and hypertrophy. Across the included trials, high loads produced greater 1RM strength gains, while measured hypertrophy was broadly similar between load conditions when training sets were taken to momentary muscular failure. The findings support load choice as goal-specific, but do not show that low-load training performed short of failure produces the same adaptations.

## Context and scope

The review searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Cochrane Library, and Scopus through March 2017 and included **21 studies**. Eligible studies were experimental trials lasting at least 6 weeks, comparing low load (**≤60% 1RM**) with high load (**>60% 1RM**), with all sets performed to momentary muscular failure. Participants were adults without known medical conditions or injuries that impaired training. The included samples varied in age, sex, and training status; the review does not represent one pooled single-trial population. Where intensity was reported by repetition maximum rather than percentage of 1RM, the authors classified sets of **15RM or fewer** as high load and sets above **15RM** as low load.

## Relevant findings

- **Review finding—1RM strength:** In 14 studies (84 effect sizes), high-load training produced significantly greater 1RM gains than low-load training (**p = 0.003**). The authors reported mean gains of about **35.4%** for high loads and **28.0%** for low loads. Low-load training nevertheless produced substantial 1RM improvement. The high-load advantage is consistent with greater specificity to maximal-load testing.
- **Review finding—other strength tests:** The analysis found no significant high-versus-low-load difference in isometric strength (**p = 0.43**). There were too few studies to model load effects reliably for isokinetic strength. The authors also judged the data insufficient for quantitative load comparisons for lean body mass and muscle-fiber size.
- **Review finding—hypertrophy:** Across 10 studies and 41 effect sizes, muscle-size measures did not differ significantly between high and low loads in the study-level analysis (**p = 0.56**). The mean percentage changes reported were **8.3%** for high-load and **7.0%** for low-load conditions. A separate mean-effect comparison showed only a statistical trend favoring high load (**p = 0.10**); sensitivity analyses showed that some studies influenced this comparison. The overall interpretation is similar hypertrophy across the studied loading ranges when sets are performed with high effort to failure, not proof that every load or protocol is equivalent.
- **Authors' practical interpretation:** For maximal strength in lifts tested by 1RM, the authors favor high loads because of specificity. They describe flexibility across loading ranges for hypertrophy, provided effort is high. Low-load sets to failure can require more repetitions, work, and time. The authors explicitly caution that their evidence cannot establish equivalent results for nonfailure training.
- **Limitations:** Only three included studies examined dynamic-strength changes in trained participants, limiting conclusions for experienced lifters. Study protocols differed; two studies had load conditions that also differed in rest duration or repetition tempo, potentially confounding hypertrophy results. Evidence was inadequate for several outcome classes, including isokinetic strength and lean mass. The review included only studies requiring momentary failure, so findings should not be generalized to submaximal sets without further evidence.
