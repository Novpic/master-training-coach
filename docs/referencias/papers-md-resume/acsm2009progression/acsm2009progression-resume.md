# Summary — acsm2009progression

## Central idea

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) position stand *Progression Models in Resistance Training for Healthy Adults* (2009) synthesizes evidence and panel recommendations on how resistance-training variables can be progressed toward distinct goals. Its central message is to match overload, exercise selection, loading, volume, rest, velocity, and frequency to the trainee’s goal, experience, and recovery rather than applying one program to everyone.

## Context and scope

This is a position stand, not a new trial or a systematic review reporting one pooled estimate. It replaces ACSM’s 2002 position stand and brings together research on strength, hypertrophy, muscular power, local muscular endurance, sports performance, and healthy older adults. It has no single study sample, comparator, or intervention duration. The recommendations are the panel’s evidence-graded guidance; grades A–D follow the paper’s stated NHLBI framework and are not a new evidence appraisal here.

The scope is healthy adults, including novice (untrained or without recent resistance-training experience), intermediate (approximately six months of consistent training), and advanced trainees (years of experience); older adults receive separate guidance. The paper emphasizes that program decisions depend on the person’s goals, capacity, training status, equipment, and recovery. Its recommendations should not be assumed to apply unchanged to people with disease or orthopedic limitations.

## Relevant findings

- **Strength — author recommendations:** For novice-to-intermediate trainees, the detailed strength section recommends 60–70% of one-repetition maximum (1RM) for 8–12 repetitions (evidence grade A); the position-stand summary describes novice loading as 8–12RM. For advanced trainees, the detailed recommendation is to cycle loads of 80–100% 1RM (grade A). The summary describes a broader periodized 1–12RM range, eventually emphasizing 1–6RM with 3–5 minutes between sets and moderate repetition timing (about 1–2 seconds concentric and 1–2 seconds eccentric). These are differently scoped summaries, not a single universal loading prescription.
- For trainees progressing at a specific RM load, the panel recommends a 2–10% load increase—lower for small-muscle-mass exercises and higher for large-muscle-mass exercises—after the trainee exceeds the target by 1–2 repetitions on two consecutive sessions (grade B). Initially, novices may use 1–3 sets per exercise (grade A); progression toward intermediate and advanced training uses multiple sets with systematic variation of volume and intensity, avoiding abrupt volume increases (grade B). Include concentric, eccentric, and isometric actions and unilateral/bilateral, single-/multiple-joint exercises; emphasize multiple-joint work for overall strength (grade A). Exercise order recommendations prioritize large before small muscle groups, multiple-joint before single-joint, and heavier/higher-intensity exercises earlier (grade C).
- For strength sessions, the detailed rest recommendation is at least 2–3 minutes for heavy core lifts and 1–2 minutes for assistance exercises (grades B and C, respectively). The summary attaches 3–5 minutes to the eventual emphasis on heavy 1–6RM work, while the detailed guidance gives at least 2–3 minutes for heavy core lifts; these scopes are not explicitly reconciled. Weekly frequency is 2–3 days for novices (grade A); 3–4 days for intermediate training, depending on total-body versus split format (grade B); and 4–6 days for advanced lifters in the detailed section (grade C). The summary gives 4–5 days for advanced training. These are weekly training days, not a claim that every muscle group is trained each day.
- **Hypertrophy — author recommendations:** Novice and intermediate trainees: 70–85% 1RM, 8–12 repetitions per set, and 1–3 sets per exercise (grade A). Advanced trainees: 70–100% 1RM for 1–12 repetitions and 3–6 sets per exercise in a periodized plan, with most work in the 6–12RM zone and less in the 1–6RM zone (grade C). The stand recommends 1–2 minutes’ rest for novice/intermediate training; advanced rest depends on the phase and exercise, with 2–3 minutes for heavy core lifts and 1–2 minutes for other moderate-to-high-intensity work (grade C). It notes that the evidence on rest duration and hypertrophy is less settled than for strength. Frequency broadly follows the strength-stage guidance: 2–3 days/week for novices, total-body or 4 days/week for intermediate split routines, and 4–6 days/week for advanced split routines (grades A–C).
- **Power — author recommendations:** In addition to a typical strength program, the stand recommends a power component of 1–3 sets of 3–6 repetitions at 30–60% 1RM for upper-body exercises and 0–60% 1RM for lower-body exercises, performed explosively (grade A). For more advanced progression, it describes periodized integration of heavy loading (85–100% 1RM) to develop force and explosive light-to-moderate loading (30–60% upper body; 0–60% lower body), with 3–6 sets and 1–6 repetitions (grade B). At least 2–3 minutes’ rest for core exercises and 1–2 minutes for assistance work are panel recommendations based on consensus (grade D). The paper stresses that peak-power loads differ by exercise and individual strength.
- **Local muscular endurance — author recommendations:** Novice/intermediate training uses relatively light loads for 10–15 repetitions with moderate-to-high volume (grade A). Advanced training uses multiple sets, periodized loading, 10–25 repetitions or more, and higher overall volume with lighter intensities (grade C). For high-repetition sets of 15–20 or more, the suggested rest is 1–2 minutes; for 10–15-repetition sets, less than 1 minute; circuit training uses only the time needed to move between stations (grade C). For 10–15 repetitions, intentionally slow repetitions may be used; for 15–25 or more, moderate-to-fast velocities are recommended (grade B). Frequency varies by stage and workout split as described for hypertrophy/endurance in the paper (2–3 days/week for novice total-body training; 3 total-body or 4 split days/week for intermediate; 4–6 days/week for advanced split routines; grades A–C).
- **Healthy older adults — distinct recommendation:** For strength and hypertrophy, the stand recommends single- and multiple-joint machine and free-weight exercises, 60–80% 1RM, 8–12 repetitions, 1–3 sets, 1–3 minutes’ rest, slow-to-moderate lifting velocity, and 2–3 days/week (grade A). For power, it recommends 1–3 sets of 6–10 repetitions at 30–60% 1RM with high repetition velocity (grade B). It also describes gradual progression as important; age-related health conditions or functional limitations require individual assessment rather than automatic use of these ranges.

These ranges are panel recommendations synthesized from studies with differing populations, methods, and durations—not results from one uniform protocol. The stand reports mixed findings for some topics (including set comparisons in less-trained people and agility outcomes) and identifies limited evidence for some hypertrophy variables. Its conclusion calls for goal-specific program design and competent professional judgment to support safe implementation. The paper does not establish that any one recommendation is optimal for every healthy adult or that its sports-performance guidance transfers to non-athletes without adjustment.

## Key formulas

$$
V = N_{\mathrm{reps}} \times L
$$

Here, $V$ is training volume as defined in the paper, $N_{\mathrm{reps}}$ is the total repetitions performed during the session, and $L$ is the resistance used in kilograms. This represents volume as repetition count multiplied by load (kg), not a universal accounting convention for training volume.

$$
P = F \times v
$$

Here, $P$ is muscular power, $F$ is force generation, and $v$ is movement velocity. The paper uses this relationship to explain why power training targets both force and the ability to produce force rapidly; it does not specify units in this definition.
