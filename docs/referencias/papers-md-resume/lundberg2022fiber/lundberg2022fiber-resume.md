# Summary — lundberg2022fiber

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis examined whether concurrent aerobic and strength training changes type I and type II muscle-fiber hypertrophy compared with the same strength-training prescription alone. It found a small, borderline overall attenuation in fiber hypertrophy, with imprecise, non-significant pooled estimates for each fiber type separately. A possible type I fiber difference when aerobic training involved running is preliminary and does not establish that running is worse than cycling.

## Context and scope

The authors synthesized 15 studies involving 300 healthy adults, including 153 participants in supervised concurrent training and 147 in strength training alone. Eligible interventions lasted at least 4 weeks and compared matched strength training, with or without aerobic training; the literature search covered six databases through March 15, 2021. Twelve studies used cycling and three used running. The registered review protocol was modified to focus on fiber hypertrophy. Study quality was mostly moderate; two studies were rated poor and one good. Fiber-size measurements are vulnerable to substantial biopsy-site variability, and most studies were small. The review therefore addresses histological fiber-size outcomes, which need not correspond to whole-muscle hypertrophy.

## Relevant findings

- **Meta-analysis findings:** The pooled standardized mean difference (SMD) for overall fiber hypertrophy was −0.23 (95% CI −0.46 to −0.00; p=0.050; I²=58.6%), a small estimate at the statistical threshold. Type I fibers had SMD −0.34 (−0.72 to 0.04; p=0.078; I²=70.4%); type II fibers had SMD −0.13 (−0.39 to 0.12; p=0.315; I²=29.7%). The fiber-specific pooled estimates do not independently establish attenuation.
- **Aerobic modality:** The type I running subgroup showed SMD −0.81 (95% CI −1.26 to −0.36), based on only three running studies; the cycling subgroup estimate was not statistically significant. However, the review did not find a statistically significant difference between running and cycling subgroups. The apparent running signal is therefore preliminary, not proof of a modality interaction or a universal reason to prefer cycling.
- **Other moderators:** The subgroup analyses did not detect differences by concurrent-training frequency, training status, training modality (different days, same day, or same session), or exercise order within a same-session program. These non-significant tests do not prove that the programming choices are equivalent.
- **Interpretation and limitations:** The authors conclude that a small fiber-level attenuation may occur without a corresponding change in whole-muscle hypertrophy. Many included samples were small, and fiber-size measurements had low reliability; the paper notes a 13% coefficient of variation for histochemical fiber-area measurements and cautions against relying on a single biopsy. The running subgroup is especially uncertain because it contained only three studies. These findings do not define a specific concurrent-training prescription or demonstrate an effect on whole-muscle size, strength, or performance.

## Key formulas

The meta-analysis compared changes in each group after standardizing each change by that group’s pre-training standard deviation:

$$
\mathrm{SMD}=
\frac{M_{\mathrm{post,CT}}-M_{\mathrm{pre,CT}}}{SD_{\mathrm{pre,CT}}}
-
\frac{M_{\mathrm{post,ST}}-M_{\mathrm{pre,ST}}}{SD_{\mathrm{pre,ST}}}
$$

$M$ is the measured muscle-fiber size, CT is concurrent aerobic-and-strength training, and ST is strength training alone. The SMD is unitless; in this review, a negative value indicates relatively less fiber-size adaptation with concurrent training.
