# Summary — blagrove2018strength

## Central idea

This systematic review evaluated strength training (ST) effects on physiological determinants and performance in middle- and long-distance runners. It concludes that ST often improves running economy (RE), time-trial performance and sprint qualities, while maximal oxygen uptake, blood-lactate measures and body-composition outcomes are generally unchanged. The effects were not consistent across all studies.

## Context and scope

The review searched three databases through October 2017 and included 24 studies. The paper reports 469 participants but separately gives 352 men and 96 women (448 total), leaving 21 participants unaccounted for in that breakdown; it does not explain the difference. Eligibility required at least 6 months of running experience, ST interventions lasting at least 4 weeks and a running-only control group. Triathletes and duathletes were also eligible. Studies lasted mostly 6–14 weeks, with two lasting 24 and 40 weeks. Reviewed interventions included heavy resistance training (HRT), explosive resistance training (ERT), plyometrics (PT), or combinations, sometimes with sprint training. PEDro scores were 4–6. This was a qualitative synthesis, not a pooled meta-analysis; running status, interventions, outcome protocols and control of running training varied.

## Relevant findings

- **Review findings:** RE was assessed in 20 studies. Fourteen reported significant improvements at one or more speeds (2–8%), including six with mixed significant and non-significant findings across test speeds; six studies found no significant improvement versus controls. Time-trial improvements were significant versus controls in 8 of 12 trials, with events from 1.5 to 10 km; reported improvements were generally 3–5% for middle distances and 2–4% for distances up to 10 km, but null comparisons also occurred. Three studies reported improved 20–30 m sprint speed (1.1–3.4%). Most studies found no significant change in VO₂max, blood-lactate markers or body composition. These are narrative summaries across different studies, not pooled effect estimates.
- **Programs represented:** HRT commonly used 2–6 sets of 3–10 repetitions per exercise at loads above 70% 1RM or to repetition failure. PT commonly used 1–6 exercises, 1–6 sets of 4–10 repetitions, totaling 30–228 foot contacts per session. Sprint work, when included, generally used 20–150 m repetitions over 4–12 sets at maximal effort. These describe study programs, not one proven optimal combined protocol.
- **Authors’ recommendations:** A variety of ST modalities, preferably supervised and periodized, may be used; the review suggests 2–3 sessions per week. It describes an initial progression from 1–2 to 3–6 sets and reports that two weekly sessions may be sufficient for RE gains. The authors suggest allowing more than 3 hours after high-intensity running before ST and at least 24 hours between ST and an intensive running session; these are review-based programming suggestions, not universal guarantees. Their proposed HRT emphasis initially and longer interventions for clarifying long-term effects are cautious recommendations, not direct comparisons establishing an optimal sequence or duration.
- **Limitations:** The reviewed trials were generally small and had moderate PEDro scores; reporting and control of running volume/intensity and prior ST experience were often incomplete. Outcome measures (including the speed used for RE assessment and scaling of oxygen uptake) varied, and several outcomes were based on few studies. The review cannot establish a single best ST method or guarantee performance benefit for an individual runner.
