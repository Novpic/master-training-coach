# Summary — maughan2018supplements

## Central idea

This International Olympic Committee consensus statement explains how high-performance athletes and their support teams should evaluate dietary supplements. It treats supplementation as a possible, usually small addition to an adequate nutrition plan—not a substitute for nutritional assessment—and emphasizes that benefits depend on the product, athlete, and situation, while health and anti-doping risks remain important.

## Context and scope

This is a consensus overview, not a single trial or a systematic review with a formally reported search strategy or evidence-grading system. The authors draw primarily on studies of healthy adults relevant to sport and state that data from elite athletes are largely absent. They discuss supplements used to address diagnosed nutrient deficiencies, make energy and nutrients more practical to consume, or seek direct or indirect performance benefits. Evidence quality and applicability vary across these uses; the statement notes individual variability and gaps in real-world competition, multi-supplement, and repeated-use research.

## Relevant findings

- **Authors’ consensus:** A complete nutrition assessment should precede a supplement decision. For suspected deficiency, evaluate dietary intake and appropriate clinical or biochemical indicators; indiscriminate micronutrient supplementation is not recommended. If normal foods cannot practically meet a sport-nutrition goal because of timing, preparation, storage, gut comfort, or energy-budget constraints, sports foods may be a convenient alternative.
- Only a small group of performance supplements—caffeine, creatine monohydrate, nitrate, sodium bicarbonate, and possibly beta-alanine—are described as having good or emerging evidence for benefits in particular scenarios. Their effects are not universal, and appropriate use depends on the event, protocol, individual response, safety, and legality. The authors advise thoroughly trialling a product in training or simulated competition before competition use.
- The statement cautions against assuming that “more is better.” Potential harms include adverse effects from excessive or inappropriate use, interactions or cumulative doses when products are combined, and contamination with undeclared prohibited substances. Third-party quality-assurance schemes may reduce but cannot eliminate contamination risk.
- **Evidence limitations:** Evidence for many marketed products is absent or weak, study populations and performance tests may not match elite competition, and even meta-analyses inherit limitations in the underlying studies. Responses can differ between people and between scenarios. Protein is discussed in the prose as supporting lean-mass gain when combined with resistance exercise; this is not a recommendation to use a supplement when food can meet the need.
- **Practical interpretation:** The decision pathway in the statement asks whether a real nutritional or performance need exists, whether the proposed product has relevant evidence and acceptable safety, whether a reliable source can be used, and whether a monitored trial produces a repeatable benefit. Because supplement safety, medication interactions, deficiencies, and anti-doping consequences may require specialist assessment, the authors encourage qualified sports-nutrition and medical input. This consensus document does not provide individualized clinical guidance.
