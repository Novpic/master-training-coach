# Summary — singer2024rest

## Central idea

This systematic review with Bayesian meta-analysis examined whether inter-set rest duration influences resistance-training hypertrophy when other training variables are controlled. The authors found a small, uncertain tendency favoring rests longer than 60 seconds for arm and thigh outcomes. They found no appreciable evidence that routinely extending rest beyond 90 seconds improves hypertrophy, although the network estimate for very long rests and quadriceps growth remained uncertain.

## Context and scope

The review included nine randomized studies of healthy adults, with interventions lasting 5–10 weeks and at least two resistance-training sessions per week. Eight studies involved younger adults (18–35 years) and one involved adults older than 65; six studies included untrained participants and three resistance-trained participants. The analysis covered 19 measurements (10 thigh, 6 arm, and 3 whole-body). Rest was categorized as short (≤60 s) versus longer (>60 s), and as short (≤60 s), intermediate (61–119 s), long (120–179 s), or very long (≥180 s). Studies controlled other training variables, including either sets per muscle per session or volume load. Most studies were short, and whole-body estimates often used fat-free mass as an indirect proxy for muscle hypertrophy.

## Relevant findings

- **Controlled short-versus-long comparisons:** Central estimates modestly favored longer rest for arm hypertrophy (SMD 0.13, 95% credible interval −0.27 to 0.51) and thigh hypertrophy (SMD 0.17, 95% credible interval −0.13 to 0.43). The whole-body estimate slightly favored shorter rest (SMD −0.08, 95% credible interval −0.45 to 0.29), but only three studies supplied whole-body data. All these intervals include effects in either direction; they do not establish a clear advantage.
- In the four-category network analysis, estimates tended to favor categories beyond ≤60 seconds for arm and thigh outcomes. A possible quadriceps advantage for very long (≥180 s) versus intermediate (61–119 s) or long (120–179 s) rest was uncertain; the authors noted substantial uncertainty and a non-monotonic pattern. They did not find appreciable hypertrophy differences when comparing rests above 90 seconds overall.
- **Volume-load context:** The authors proposed that rests longer than 60 seconds may help preserve repetitions and volume load. One 10-week study reported greater quadriceps cross-sectional-area increases with 180 versus 60 seconds (13.1% vs. 6.8%) when the shorter-rest condition completed fewer repetitions across three sets (9.8 ± 2.9 vs. 16.1 ± 5.2). When additional sets equated volume load, hypertrophy was similar. This is a study-level example, not a pooled prescription.
- Subanalyses did not indicate a meaningful difference in the rest-interval effect between sets taken to momentary failure and sets stopped short of failure. Evidence was insufficient to draw a reliable conclusion for trained lifters. Whole-body estimates also warrant caution because fat-free mass includes non-muscle tissues.
- **Practical interpretation:** The results support allowing enough rest to sustain intended repetitions and training work rather than imposing very short rests for hypertrophy by default. They do not establish a universal rest interval or a need to rest longer than 90 seconds.
- Important limitations include the small evidence base, substantial variation in exercise selection, mostly young and untrained samples, generally moderate training volumes, 5–10-week duration, no torso-muscle measurements, and no network-consistency testing because the evidence network was too sparse.

## Key formulas

For controlled comparisons, the authors used a bias-corrected standardized mean difference based on the difference in pre-to-post change between rest conditions:

$$
\mathrm{SMD}_{\mathrm{controlled}} = \frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{long}} - \Delta_{\mathrm{short}}}{SD_{\mathrm{pooled,pre}}}
$$

Here, $\Delta_{\mathrm{long}}$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{short}}$ are the pre-to-post changes in the longer- and shorter-rest conditions, respectively, and $SD_{\mathrm{pooled,pre}}$ is the pooled pre-intervention standard deviation. Positive estimates favor longer rest. The review applied a small-sample bias correction.
