# Summary — lauersen2014prevention

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis assessed whether exercise-based primary-prevention programs reduce sports injuries, and compared pooled effects by exercise type and injury type. The pooled results favor strength and proprioception training and multicomponent programs, but not stretching alone; the overall estimate is heterogeneous and should not be treated as a universal program effect.

## Context and scope

The authors included 25 human randomized controlled trials of initially uninjured participants, totaling 26,610 people and 3,464 injuries. The studies covered adults and adolescents, including recruits and recreational and competitive athletes, with differing sports, programs, follow-up periods, and injury definitions. Searches of PubMed, EMBASE, Web of Science, and SPORTDiscus were updated through January 2013. The authors used random-effects models and adjusted effective sample sizes for cluster randomization where needed. The review addressed prevention, not treatment or rehabilitation of an existing injury; it did not establish a single exercise dose or protocol applicable to every population or injury.

## Relevant findings

- **Review findings:** The cluster-adjusted pooled relative risk (RR) across exercise programs was 0.632 (95% CI 0.533–0.750; 25 trials; I²=70%). This corresponds to a lower pooled injury risk, but the authors described the overall result as clinically difficult to apply because effects varied substantially among studies and populations. A sensitivity analysis restricted to studies reporting intention-to-treat data produced a similar RR of 0.608 (95% CI 0.503–0.736).
- **By exercise exposure:** Strength-training programs had RR 0.315 (95% CI 0.207–0.480; 4 studies), proprioception-focused programs RR 0.550 (0.347–0.869; 6 studies), and multicomponent programs RR 0.655 (0.520–0.826; 12 studies). The multicomponent estimate was heterogeneous (I²=69%); it does not identify which component produced the effect. Stretching alone showed no statistically supported preventive effect (RR 0.963, 0.846–1.095; 3 studies). The strength estimate is promising but rests on four trials with varied populations and outcomes, not a standardized prescription.
- **By injury type:** Across the programs that reported these outcomes, acute injuries were reduced (RR 0.647, 0.502–0.836; 9 studies; I²=73%) and overuse injuries were reduced (RR 0.527, 0.373–0.746; 6 studies; I²=19%). Most overuse-injury data came from multicomponent programs, so the review could not isolate the effective component.
- **Authors’ interpretation:** Build multicomponent programs from exposures with evidence of benefit and continue research on individual exposures, particularly strength training. The forest plots show wide variation among individual trial estimates, consistent with the reported heterogeneity; they do not supply a common training prescription.
- **Limitations:** The total analysis had a significant small-study effect, which may reflect publication bias, heterogeneity, chance, or methodological differences. Some trials initially lacked cluster adjustment or intention-to-treat analysis; the authors adjusted where possible and ran sensitivity analyses. Injury outcomes and interventions varied, and some subgroup classifications were made after the literature search. These findings support cautious, population-specific prevention planning rather than a guarantee of reduced injury risk.

## Key formulas

The review used the design effect to adjust the effective sample size of cluster-randomized trials:

$$
\mathrm{IF}=1+(n-1)\rho
$$

Here, $n$ is the average cluster size, $\rho$ is the intracluster correlation coefficient, and IF is the inflation factor. The effective sample size is the original sample size divided by IF; this adjustment accounts for the non-independence of people within the same cluster.
