# Summary — robinson2024rir

## Central idea

This exploratory series of meta-regressions examined whether the estimated repetitions in reserve (RIR) at set termination relate continuously to resistance-training strength and hypertrophy outcomes. Across pooled evidence, RIR showed little association with strength gain, while muscle-size gains tended to be greater when sets were estimated to finish closer to failure. Because RIR was inferred for most study groups rather than measured, the results do not establish a precise optimal RIR prescription.

## Context and scope

The authors assembled 55 studies from existing meta-analyses and additional studies; this was not a systematic search and was not preregistered. Eligible comparisons used load-equated conditions (within ±5% of 1RM), equated set and/or repetition volume, compared at least two proximities to failure, and assessed maximal strength and/or direct muscle hypertrophy in participants without known injury or medical conditions. The interventions averaged 8.28 ± 2.35 weeks; participants averaged 27.83 ± 12.84 years. Strength models included 243 effects from 55 studies; hypertrophy models included 140 effects from 26 studies. The most common program exposures were six sets per muscle group per week, two sessions per week, and approximately 75% 1RM for strength outcomes; mean load was about 72% 1RM in both outcome sets. RIR was estimated from failure descriptions, velocity loss, reported RIR, load/repetitions, or alternative set structures, then averaged across relevant sets. Multilevel models adjusted for load, volume-equating method, intervention duration, and training status.

## Relevant findings

- **Strength:** The marginal estimated-RIR slopes had confidence intervals containing zero across the best-fit models, indicating no clear relationship between RIR and strength gains at a given load. The authors suggest load specificity and counterbalancing effects—fatigue management farther from failure versus practice with heavier, near-maximal effort closer to failure—as possible explanations; these are interpretations, not experimentally isolated mechanisms.
- **Hypertrophy:** In the best-fit models, the slope was negative for estimated RIR (e.g., standardized mean change model β = −0.019, 95% CI −0.035 to −0.004), consistent with greater muscle-size gains as sets ended closer to failure. The association remained in sensitivity analyses excluding estimated values above 10 RIR. The authors do not identify a single threshold that maximizes hypertrophy, and the result should not be read as proof that every set should reach failure.
- **Exposure and interpretation:** The analyzed hypertrophy programs averaged 9.69 sets per muscle group per week, 72.27% 1RM, and 2.08 sessions per week. The pooled relation applies to volume-equated study conditions; it may differ when set volume is adjusted for recovery or when program dose is changed.
- This is exploratory, population-average evidence with modest overall model fit and broad uncertainty. The estimated RIR values’ accuracy is unknown; individual repetition capacity, set-to-set fatigue, strength progression, exercise choice, and failure definitions could misclassify actual proximity to failure. Many studies lacked data, and average RIR obscures variation across sets and sessions. The authors explicitly caution against individual-level or universal prescription from these estimates.

## Key formulas

The paper defines relative volume load as repetitions multiplied by sets and relative load:

$$\text{Relative volume load} = \text{repetitions} \times \text{sets} \times \%\text{1RM}$$

Here %1RM is the load expressed as a fraction or percentage of one-repetition maximum. The authors use this quantity to explain why velocity-loss conditions can differ in both proximity to failure and volume load.
