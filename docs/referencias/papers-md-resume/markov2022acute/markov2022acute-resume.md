# Summary — markov2022acute

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the immediate effect of aerobic exercise performed before strength testing on subsequent lower-limb strength and power in trained males. On average, aerobic exercise was followed by a moderate reduction in strength but no statistically detectable pooled change in power. The results apply to acute responses, not long-term concurrent-training adaptations.

## Context and scope

Fifteen within-person repeated-measures studies, with 197 trained male participants aged 18–42 years, were included. The aerobic exposures were running, cycling, or sport-specific small-sided soccer; eligible post-exercise measurements were taken within 15 minutes. Strength outcomes were mainly knee-extensor tests, while power was assessed with jump measures. The review searched PubMed, Web of Science, and Google Scholar through July 2021. Aerobic protocols varied in intensity, duration, and continuity; only a small number of studies informed several subgroup analyses. The authors rated study quality from 7 to 11 on their 14-point tool.

## Relevant findings

- **Pooled acute effects:** Aerobic exercise was followed by lower strength (SMD 0.79, 95% CI 0.38–1.21; p=0.003; 8 studies; I²=15%). For power, the pooled change was not statistically significant (SMD 0.04, −0.39 to 0.46; p=0.846; 9 studies; I²=60%). The power result is uncertain rather than evidence that every power task is unaffected; individual studies reported both decreases and post-activation performance enhancement.
- **Intensity and duration:** Moderate-to-high-intensity aerobic exercise was associated with a strength decline (SMD 0.65, 0.16–1.13; 5 studies), while the low-intensity estimate was imprecise and not significant (SMD 0.65, −1.45 to 2.75; 2 studies). The between-subgroup difference was not significant (p=0.979). Exercise lasting >30 minutes had SMD 1.02 (0.01–2.03; 4 studies), and exercise lasting ≤30 minutes had SMD 0.59 (0.23–0.95; 4 studies); their subgroup difference was also not significant (p=0.204). These data do not establish intensity- or duration-dependent effects.
- **Exercise mode:** Cycling was associated with a larger acute strength decrement (SMD 0.79, 0.45–1.13; 5 studies) than running (SMD 0.28, 0.08–0.47; 2 studies); the subgroup difference was significant (p<0.0001). The authors note that all strength tests were knee-extensor tests, so local cycling fatigue may contribute to this contrast. This finding concerns these trained male samples and immediate testing; it conflicts with some prior long-term concurrent-training evidence and should not be generalized as a universal modality ranking.
- **Authors’ practical interpretation:** If aerobic exercise must precede strength exercise in a trained male’s session, the results may inform session planning, but the study does not test a long-term program or provide individualized advice. The strongest general finding is acute strength reduction when aerobic exercise comes first; exact response varies with protocol, test, and training background.
- **Limitations:** The evidence is restricted to trained males and cannot be extrapolated to females or youth. Protocols were heterogeneous, mechanistic physiological measures were generally unavailable, and several subgroup estimates relied on few studies. The meta-analysis measured strength and power within 15 minutes; some individual studies discussed longer-lasting responses, but those time points were not part of the pooled analysis.
