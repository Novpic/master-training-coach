# Summary — rodriguez2009nutrition

## Central idea

This joint position statement from the American Dietetic Association, Dietitians of Canada, and the American College of Sports Medicine summarizes evidence and panel recommendations on nutrition for active adults and athletes. Its central message is to meet energy, carbohydrate, protein, fluid, and micronutrient needs in a context-sensitive way, while treating body-composition measurements and supplements cautiously.

## Context and scope

This is an evidence-based position statement, not a single intervention study. Its evidence analysis drew on PubMed, CENTRAL, related research, and reviews, with the principal search period spanning 1995–2006. The stated evidence-analysis population was adults aged 18–40 years in sport settings, including trained athletes, athletes in training, and regularly exercising people. The organizations graded conclusion statements from I (good) to V (no evidence to support or refute); these are the authors’ evidence grades, not a current independent appraisal. The statement records that its position was in effect through December 31, 2012. Its recommendations and supplement/regulatory discussion should therefore be read as historical guidance, not assumed to represent current standards.

## Relevant findings

- **Authors’ general recommendations:** Meet energy needs during high-intensity or long-duration training; low energy intake can compromise health, training adaptation, and recovery. The statement discourages daily weigh-ins and use of body weight or composition as the sole criterion for sport participation. Body-composition methods have meaningful error, so individualized assessment and ranges—not rigid percentage targets—are emphasized.
- **Macronutrients:** The position gives carbohydrate guidance of 6–10 g/kg body weight/day, adjusted for energy expenditure, sport, sex, and environment. Its protein ranges distinguish endurance athletes (1.2–1.4 g/kg/day) from strength-trained athletes (approximately 1.2–1.7 g/kg/day); the authors state these intakes can generally be achieved through food. Fat is recommended at 20–35% of total energy. These are broad athlete-focused recommendations from this 2009 statement, not individualized prescriptions.
- **Before, during, and after exercise:** For pre-exercise hydration, the statement recommends about 5–7 mL/kg at least 4 hours before exercise. It recommends approximately 30–60 g carbohydrate/hour during prolonged endurance exercise, especially when pre-exercise intake is inadequate or conditions are demanding. For rapid recovery after substantial fluid loss, it describes 450–675 mL per 0.5 kg of body mass lost. When rapid glycogen restoration is needed, it gives 1.0–1.5 g carbohydrate/kg within 30 minutes after exercise and again every 2 hours for 4–6 hours; it notes that precise timing is less important when the next hard session is a day or more away and adequate daily carbohydrate is consumed. Fluid intake should not exceed sweat losses; the paper warns both dehydration and excessive water intake can be harmful.
- **Evidence grades:** The statement assigns Grade II (fair) to its evidence conclusions on training diet, pre-exercise meals, during-exercise fueling, and recovery; its conclusion on energy restriction and weight-class athletes is Grade III (limited). For example, the pre-event meal evidence was mixed, whereas during-exercise carbohydrate findings were more favorable for events longer than 60 minutes than for shorter events.
- The position recommends individualized nutrition assessment, particularly for weight change, vegetarian diets, micronutrient concerns, and supplements. It notes that adequate diets generally do not require routine vitamin/mineral supplements and urges careful assessment of supplement safety, efficacy, potency, and legality. Clinical issues such as iron deficiency or disordered eating require qualified professional review rather than self-prescription.

## Key formulas

Energy availability is defined in the paper as dietary energy remaining after exercise expenditure, normalized to fat-free mass:

$$\text{Energy availability} = \frac{\text{dietary energy intake} - \text{exercise energy expenditure}}{\text{fat-free mass}}$$

The paper expresses this as kcal·kg FFM⁻¹·day⁻¹ and discusses 30 kcal·kg FFM⁻¹·day⁻¹ as a proposed lower threshold for females, not as a universal diagnostic cutoff. This is a conceptual estimate; the position statement notes that energy-needs calculations are approximations.
