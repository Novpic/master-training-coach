# Summary — kreider2017creatine

## Central idea

This ISSN position stand reviews creatine monohydrate’s efficacy and safety in sport, exercise, and selected clinical contexts. The authors identify creatine monohydrate as an evidence-supported aid for increasing muscle creatine stores and improving high-intensity or repeated-effort capacity and training adaptations; they distinguish this athletic evidence from more preliminary or mixed clinical applications.

## Context and scope

This is a broad narrative review and society position stand, not a single experiment or a systematic meta-analysis. It summarizes research across healthy trained and untrained people, athletes, older adults, adolescents, and clinical populations. The authors report that much of the sport evidence concerns men; benefits and study certainty vary by population and outcome. The paper was prepared as the official ISSN position and reports Council for Responsible Nutrition support as well as multiple author-industry relationships. Its broad safety conclusions apply chiefly to studied doses and populations and should not be treated as individualized medical advice.

## Relevant findings

- **ISSN position—exercise and performance:** Creatine monohydrate can increase muscle creatine/phosphocreatine and support high-intensity, repeated sprint or set performance, potentially allowing more training work and greater strength or lean-mass adaptations. The review reports that high-intensity/repetitive performance after loading is generally increased by about 10–20%, depending on the increase in muscle phosphocreatine; this is a broad summary across studies, not an expected individual response. Benefits are most relevant to strength-power and intermittent high-intensity activities. Evidence is less uniform for endurance-only performance, and the review notes that women are underrepresented and may show different training responses.
- **ISSN position—adult supplementation protocol:** The rapid-loading approach is approximately 0.3 g/kg/day of creatine monohydrate for 5–7 days (described in the review as about 5 g four times daily), followed by 3–5 g/day to maintain elevated stores. Some larger athletes may require 5–10 g/day for maintenance. An alternative is 3–5 g/day without loading; muscle creatine rises more gradually, with full saturation over roughly 3–4 weeks described in the position statement. The authors state that taking creatine with carbohydrate, or carbohydrate plus protein, can increase retention, but do not establish that this necessarily improves performance more than creatine alone.
- **Review findings—safety:** Weight gain is the most consistently reported side effect. In healthy trained and untrained people, the reviewed evidence did not show a compelling increase in renal dysfunction, dehydration, cramping, gastrointestinal upset, or injury at studied doses; short- and long-term studies include periods up to several years. These findings do not establish safety for every dose or every medical condition. The article notes that some case reports involved elevated creatinine or renal concerns, that clinical conditions and disease populations differ, and that consultation is prudent for people with pre-existing renal disease.
- **Population-specific caveats:** The ISSN’s discussion of use by younger athletes restricts its recommendation to serious, supervised training, an adequate diet, knowledge of appropriate use, and adherence to recommended doses. The cited clinical-disease regimens (including high doses) are not general sport protocols. The review also reports that large trials found no clinical outcome benefit for Parkinson’s disease or ALS; pregnancy-related benefits discussed were mainly animal research and hypotheses, not a basis for a human recommendation.
