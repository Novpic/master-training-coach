# Summary — refalo2025sex

## Central idea

This systematic review and Bayesian meta-analysis compared resistance-training-related changes in muscle size between healthy adult males and females, distinguishing raw (absolute) gains from percentage (relative) gains. The overall evidence indicates a small male advantage in absolute change but similar relative hypertrophy, consistent with broadly similar proportional muscle-growth responses from different average starting muscle sizes.

## Context and scope

Eligible studies compared healthy male and female adults aged 18–45 years who completed the same resistance-training intervention. The review screened 2,720 records; 30 studies met inclusion criteria, but data from one could not be retrieved, leaving 29 studies in the meta-analysis. The analysis included 68 muscle-size outcomes and 1,278 male and 1,537 female data points (outcomes, not counts of unique participants). Interventions lasted 6–24 weeks, with a mean duration of 11 weeks. Measures included direct local muscle assessments and indirect whole-body estimates such as DXA lean mass and bioelectrical impedance. Bayesian hierarchical models reported effect estimates, highest-density credible intervals (HDIs), and probability of direction. Training dose and proximity-to-failure subgroup analyses were omitted because intervention reporting did not support reliable extraction.

## Relevant findings

- **Review finding—overall change:** Absolute hypertrophy slightly favored males (SMD 0.19, 95% HDI 0.11–0.28; posterior probability of direction 100%). Relative change was similar between sexes: the exponentiated log response ratio (lnRR) indicated a 0.69% difference favoring males, with a 95% HDI from −1.50% to 2.88%. This small estimated relative difference is uncertain and compatible with effects in either direction.
- **Body region:** Relative changes were similar in upper- and lower-body outcomes. The paper’s abstract says the absolute advantage favored males for upper-body but not lower-body regions; the discussion instead compares absolute estimates of 0.30 for upper body and 0.17 for lower body, describing the upper-body difference as larger. The lower-body subgroup interpretation is therefore inconsistent across sections; the robust summary is that the absolute difference was larger in the upper body, while relative changes were similar across regions.
- **Muscle-fibre outcomes:** A possible greater type I fibre hypertrophy in males was reported, but these secondary analyses involved seven studies and 170 participants, with wide uncertainty and methodological variation in biopsy sampling and fibre classification. Type II fibre changes were described as similar. These subgroup findings are tentative, not a basis for sex-specific prescriptions.
- **Authors’ practical application:** The authors suggest broadly similar resistance-training structures can be used across sexes, with programming choices guided by goals, preferences, and individual circumstances. This is an interpretation of pooled averages, not evidence that individuals respond identically.
- Limitations include sparse evidence in resistance-trained participants (six studies), unclear training experience in some samples, variation in intervention and assessment methods, and assumed pre-to-post correlation (*r* = 0.87) for the primary SMD analysis. Sensitivity analyses using *r* = 0.70–0.99 had little effect on the pooled SMD. DXA/BIA lean-mass estimates may be affected by fluid changes and should not be equated directly with skeletal-muscle mass.
