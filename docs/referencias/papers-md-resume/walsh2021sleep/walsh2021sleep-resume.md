# Summary — walsh2021sleep

## Central idea

This narrative review and expert consensus summarizes sleep-related evidence for athletes and offers a practitioner toolbox for education, screening, sleep opportunity, and referral. The authors favor individualized sleep management rather than assuming a single target fits every athlete; they also emphasize that evidence on partial sleep restriction, sleep extension, and athlete-specific outcomes remains incomplete.

## Context and scope

This is a narrative review and consensus statement, not a new intervention trial or systematic meta-analysis. Its focus is predominantly elite athletes, drawing on research with varied populations, methods, and sleep measures. The panel reviews sleep adequacy, performance, athlete-specific risk factors, sleep measurement, travel and circadian issues, nutrition, and applied strategies. The authors identify inconsistent methods, underrepresentation of female athletes, and limited validity of some sleep measures as major constraints on the evidence.

## Relevant findings

- The review describes habitual sleep under 7 hours per night and fragmented or poor-quality sleep as common markers of inadequacy in elite-athlete literature. Studies reported sleep disturbance in 50%–78% of elite athletes and highly disturbed sleep in 22%–26%; these are ranges across heterogeneous studies, not a single population estimate.
- Complete sleep deprivation (a night or more without sleep) is associated with impaired athletic performance, but results for partial restriction over 1–3 nights and acute sleep improvement are mixed. Effects vary with protocol, test timing, and outcome; some skill, submaximal-strength, and anaerobic-power measures may decline while maximal aerobic or strength efforts may be maintained.
- Sleep education sessions lasting 30–60 minutes have been reported to increase sleep duration by 20–90 minutes, although one study found the improvement was not maintained at one-month follow-up. The authors suggest repeated, sport-relevant education and follow-up rather than assuming a single session has lasting effects.
- The consensus cites short naps under 30 minutes as a possible way to support alertness and performance. It discusses mid-afternoon timing (13:00–16:00) and 15–20-minute “coffee naps”; caffeine can also disrupt sleep, so this is not a blanket recommendation and should be considered in light of the athlete’s sleep pattern.
- Examples of sleep extension in the reviewed literature include a 10-hour time-in-bed opportunity each night for 5–7 weeks in collegiate basketball players, associated with improvements in reaction time, sprint times, mood, and free-throw accuracy. The authors also describe a small cyclist/triathlete study with three nights at about 8.4 versus 6.8 hours of sleep. These findings are preliminary and do not establish that all athletes need a fixed extension target.
- **Consensus guidance:** 7–9 hours is presented as an appropriate range for healthy adults and 8–10 hours for teenagers, while the panel cautions against imposing a one-size-fits-all athlete target and advises considering perceived sleep needs and individual circumstances. The toolbox recommends screening with athlete-validated tools and prompt sleep-professional referral for moderate-to-severe sleep problems; generic “sleep more” advice can be unsuitable, including for someone with insomnia.
- Consumer sleep devices and apps may misestimate sleep and can increase anxiety or preoccupation. The authors recommend considering validation, interpreting measurements cautiously, and weighing potential privacy concerns.
- Sleep disturbance is associated with intensified training and overreaching, but its direction of causation is unresolved. In the reviewed literature, reported changes were often modest (sleep efficiency reduction under 5% and sleep duration reduction under 30 minutes); larger changes were described in an individual case and cannot establish a general pattern.
- Nutrition evidence is limited. The review reports that caffeine often worsens sleep measures, while evidence for food-based or supplement approaches in athletes is too sparse for firm conclusions; the authors caution against unsubstantiated sleep-supplement claims.
