# Summary — garber2011exercise

## Central idea

This ACSM position stand synthesizes evidence available through 2010 into individualized exercise guidance for apparently healthy adults. Its main message is that a comprehensive program combining cardiorespiratory, resistance, flexibility, and neuromotor exercise can improve or maintain health and fitness; the dose should be adjusted to the person’s goals, habitual activity, health, physical function, and response.

## Context and scope

This is an evidence-based professional position stand, not a single trial. The writing group reviewed epidemiological studies, randomized and nonrandomized trials, meta-analyses, guidelines, consensus statements, and scientific reviews published since the 1998 ACSM stand. Observational research supplies much of the evidence linking activity to health outcomes, while exercise trials and meta-analyses inform training effects. The recommendations principally concern adults seeking general health and fitness, not competitive athletes using advanced training. Applying them to people with chronic disease or disability requires suitable assessment and professional advice.

## Relevant findings

- **ACSM recommendations—cardiorespiratory exercise:** For most adults, the stand recommends moderate-intensity exercise for at least 30 minutes per day on at least 5 days per week (at least 150 minutes per week), or vigorous-intensity exercise for at least 20 minutes per day on at least 3 days per week (at least 75 minutes per week), or a combination of moderate and vigorous activity. It also states a target total expenditure of 500–1000 MET·min per week. These are targets, not prerequisites for benefit: less exercise may still help, particularly for previously sedentary people. Gradual increases in duration, frequency, and/or intensity are advised until the individual’s maintenance goal is reached.
- **ACSM recommendations—resistance training for general fitness:** Train each major muscle group 2–3 days per week. The prose recommends 60–80% of one-repetition maximum (1RM) overall; novices and intermediate trainees are directed to 60–70% 1RM, while experienced trainees may use at least 80% 1RM. A typical strength/power prescription is 8–12 repetitions per set and 2–4 sets per muscle group, with 2–3 minutes between sets and 48–72 hours before training the same muscle group again. The load should allow the target repetitions to produce fatigue but not exhaustion. For muscular endurance, the stand describes loads below 50% 1RM for 15–25 repetitions and no more than two sets. Older, very deconditioned, or frail adults starting resistance training may begin around 40–50% 1RM for 10–20 repetitions; the authors describe three sets of 8–12 repetitions at 20–50% 1RM as a promising power-training example for older adults, while noting that definitive power guidelines need further study. Progression is gradual and may use greater resistance, more repetitions, and/or higher frequency.
- **ACSM recommendations—flexibility:** Exercise the major muscle–tendon groups at least 2–3 days per week; static stretches are generally held for 10–30 seconds, while 30–60 seconds may benefit older adults. Repeat each stretch 2–4 times, aiming for about 60 seconds total stretch time per exercise. Stretch to tightness or slight discomfort. Stretching can transiently impair subsequent strength or power performance; for general fitness, the authors suggest doing it after cardiorespiratory or resistance exercise or as a separate session. The review does not establish that regular flexibility exercise prevents injury, low-back pain, or delayed-onset muscle soreness.
- **ACSM recommendations—neuromotor training:** For older adults, activities involving balance, agility, coordination, gait, and proprioception—including multifaceted practices such as tai chi or yoga—can support physical function and reduce falls in those at risk. Programs reporting benefits commonly used 2–3 sessions per week of 20–30 minutes. Evidence and effective dose are less established for younger and middle-aged adults; the optimal intensity, volume, pattern, and progression remain unknown.
- **Evidence qualifications:** Health benefits are associated with physical activity and fitness, but response varies substantially between people. Fitness intensity should be interpreted relative to individual capacity where possible. The stand emphasizes that exercise below the stated targets may still be beneficial, and reducing sedentary time is a separate goal. Its safety guidance favors screening for warning signs, appropriate warm-up/cool-down, gradual progression, and medical assessment/testing when clinically indicated—not routine testing for everyone.
