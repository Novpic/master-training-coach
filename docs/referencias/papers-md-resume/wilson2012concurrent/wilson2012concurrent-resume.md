# Summary — wilson2012concurrent

## Central idea

This meta-analysis examined which endurance-training characteristics were associated with smaller strength-training adaptations during concurrent training. It reported that lower-body power was more susceptible to attenuation than strength or hypertrophy overall, and that running-based concurrent training was associated with smaller lower-body strength and hypertrophy adaptations than strength training alone. Study-level associations with endurance frequency and session duration suggest possible volume-related interference, but they are not causal dose-response tests.

## Context and scope

The review pooled 21 primary studies and 422 effect sizes, including comparisons of strength-only, endurance-only, and concurrent programs, as well as some comparisons between concurrent programs. Eligible studies measured at least one strength, power, or hypertrophy outcome and had comparable baseline participant characteristics between groups. Participants varied in sex, age, and training status; the authors characterize the samples as primarily recreationally and strength trained. The literature search covered studies from 1980 through February 2011. The reported effect sizes were based on within-group pre-to-post change; they are standardized adaptation magnitudes, not direct units of strength or muscle gain.

## Relevant findings

- **Overall effect-size summaries:** Mean hypertrophy effect sizes were 1.23 for strength-only training and 0.85 for concurrent training; the authors reported no significant overall difference between these groups. Mean strength effect sizes were 1.76 and 1.44, respectively, with no significant overall difference reported. For lower-body power, mean effect sizes were 0.91 with strength-only training and 0.55 with concurrent training; the authors reported significant differences among strength-only, endurance-only, and concurrent groups. Power pooled immediate and mean-power tests because there were too few studies to analyze them separately.
- **Modality and body region:** In the running subgroup, the within-group hypertrophy effect size was 0.68 with concurrent training (95% CI 0.31–1.06; n=16) versus 1.54 with strength-only training (95% CI 1.10–1.97; n=12); the authors reported a significant difference. For lower-body strength, the corresponding effect sizes were 1.23 (95% CI 0.81–1.65; n=9) and 2.22 (95% CI 1.70–2.74; n=14), respectively, also reported as significantly different. The review did not find significant upper-body decrements in its predominantly lower-body endurance-training context. These moderator results came from limited subsets and do not prove that cycling prevents interference or that running universally causes it.
- Across studies, endurance frequency correlated negatively with lower-body hypertrophy, strength, and power effect sizes (reported correlations approximately −0.26 to −0.35); average endurance-session duration also correlated negatively (approximately −0.29 to −0.75). These study-level correlations cannot determine individual dose thresholds or establish causation. The authors noted that they did not analyze total weekly frequency of work for each muscle group across both training modes.
- **Authors’ practical suggestions:** For athletes prioritizing maximal power or rate of force development, the authors advised limiting concurrent endurance and strength work. They also suggested avoiding endurance bouts longer than about 20–30 minutes when performed more than 3 days per week. These are recommendations from a 2012 synthesis, not experimentally established universal cutoffs; the findings are moderator associations across study protocols, and sport-specific examples are illustrative.
- **Limitations:** The synthesis used a relatively small, heterogeneous set of older studies and multiple effect sizes from the same 21 studies. Moderator associations may reflect study-level differences and sparse data. The total frequency of muscle-group training was not analyzed. Treat the results as historical, hypothesis-generating guidance rather than an individualized prescription.

## Key formulas

The study calculated a pre-to-post standardized effect size as:

$$
ES = \frac{\bar{x}_{\mathrm{post}} - \bar{x}_{\mathrm{pre}}}{SD_{\mathrm{pre}}}
$$

Here, $\bar{x}_{\mathrm{post}}$ and $\bar{x}_{\mathrm{pre}}$ are the post-training and pre-training group means, and $SD_{\mathrm{pre}}$ is the pre-training standard deviation. The authors then applied a small-sample bias correction; the exact correction is not reproduced here.
