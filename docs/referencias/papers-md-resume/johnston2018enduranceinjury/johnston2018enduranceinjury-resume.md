# Summary — johnston2018enduranceinjury

## Central idea

This prospectively registered systematic review examined whether training load and baseline characteristics were associated with later musculoskeletal injury or pain in endurance sport populations. Across a small and methodologically varied prospective literature, some external-load measures and prior injury history were associated with injury or pain, but the findings are observational and do not establish causal or universal training thresholds.

## Context and scope

The review included 12 prospective cohort studies of adults aged 18–65 in running, rowing, and triathlon, with recreational and elite participants followed for more than three months (follow-up ranged from three months to three years). It searched eight databases and rated study quality and risk of bias using CASP. Injury and pain definitions, surveillance schedules, training measures, and analyses differed substantially, so the authors did not pool results in a meta-analysis. Injury reporting was often subjective, and several studies did not address confounding. Nine studies involved recreational populations, three elite populations, and one included both; most data therefore should not be generalized beyond the studied endurance settings.

## Relevant findings

- **Review findings—external training load:** Higher total training distance was associated with injury or pain in three of six studies that statistically analyzed the relationship; other studies found no significant association. Two recreational-running cohorts associated fewer than two sessions per week with higher injury rates (one reported HR 2.41, 95% CI 1.71–3.42); one study also reported higher risk among female recreational runners completing fewer than one run per week. A single recreational-running cohort associated less than two training hours per week with injury (HR 3.29, 95% CI 2.27–4.79). Among elite rowers, higher monthly training hours correlated with more new low-back-pain episodes (r = 0.83), but the relevant hours threshold was not specified. These are study-specific associations, not dose prescriptions; the review also found null results for several other load measures, including a weekly running-distance increase greater than 30% versus less than 10% in one cohort.
- **Review findings—baseline characteristics:** Previous injury was the most consistently reported characteristic, associated with subsequent injury or pain in five studies, generally with effects the authors classified as medium; at least one study found no significant association. Age above approximately 45 years was associated with a small increase in risk in some cohorts, while non-musculoskeletal comorbidity was associated with persistent post-marathon injury or pain in one recreational-running cohort. Older shoes in female runners and noncompetitive (“Type B”) behavior were reported as possible associations in single studies. The behavior result is uncertain: its reported confidence interval crossed the null despite a reported p = 0.04, and the authors noted conflicting findings in other running literature. BMI was not significantly associated with injury or pain in the studies that examined it.
- **Authors’ practical interpretation:** Monitor and progress training distance, volume, and frequency gradually, and consider individual history such as previous injury. This is a cautious implication of observational evidence, not a validated universal progression rule or a clinical risk assessment.
- **Key evidence gap:** None of the included studies reported internal training-load measures or acute:chronic workload ratios. The review therefore cannot establish how athletes’ physiological or perceived responses to training interact with external workload and injury risk.
