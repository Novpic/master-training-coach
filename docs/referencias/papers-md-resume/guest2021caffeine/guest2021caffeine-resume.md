# Summary — guest2021caffeine

## Central idea

This International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand critically reviews research on caffeine and exercise and states the Society’s practical positions. Caffeine can acutely improve several performance outcomes, with the most consistent evidence for aerobic endurance, but response and adverse effects vary; the document therefore frames its dose and timing ranges as evidence-based guidance rather than a guarantee for every person or event.

## Context and scope

This is a society position stand and broad literature review, not a new intervention study or a single pooled analysis conducted by the authors. It summarizes findings across exercise modes, delivery forms, and populations, including trained and untrained adults, while noting substantial variability and mixed results in some areas. Several authors disclose relationships with supplement or sports-nutrition companies, and the ISSN has received support from supplement companies; interpret the recommendations with that context in mind.

## Relevant findings

- **ISSN position—dose and timing:** The Society identifies 3–6 mg caffeine/kg body mass as the range most consistently associated with improved performance; the minimal effective dose is uncertain and may be as low as 2 mg/kg. Around 60 minutes pre-exercise is the most commonly studied timing, not a universal optimum. Faster caffeine delivery from chewing gum may permit a shorter wait. Very high doses such as 9 mg/kg do not appear necessary for an ergogenic effect and are associated with more side effects.
- **Endurance:** The review describes endurance as the most consistent performance domain, with studies commonly reporting roughly 2–4% improvement at 3–6 mg/kg. A cited meta-analysis of 46 randomized placebo-controlled time-trial studies reported mean-power improvement of 2.9% (SD 2.2%; ES 0.22) and completion-time improvement of 2.3% (SD 2.6%; ES 0.28), while some studies found slower times or lower power. A separate synthesis reported a broad range of individual trial responses, underscoring that the average is not a prediction for every athlete.
- **Strength, muscular endurance, power, and sport tasks:** The review reports small average effects on maximal strength (meta-analytic ESs about 0.16–0.20, or roughly 2–7%) and modest benefits in some muscular-endurance and jumping outcomes, but findings for muscular endurance and repeated-sprint power are inconsistent. Caffeine can improve some movement-velocity, throwing, jumping, and sport-specific outcomes; laboratory effects do not invariably transfer to match performance. The source describes no universal response across tasks or athletes.
- **Heat and altitude:** The Society considers caffeine-supported endurance exercise in heat (3–6 mg/kg) and at altitude (4–6 mg/kg) supported by the available literature. The review notes that results in heat are mixed and altitude evidence is limited, so these contexts should not be treated as established guarantees or generalized beyond the studied conditions.
- **Individual response and trade-offs:** Training status and habitual intake do not consistently explain the response. Genetic differences are a possible contributor, but the review describes conflicting genotype findings and does not support routine genetic testing as a practical selector. Caffeine may impair sleep or increase anxiety, palpitations, or jitters; effects are dose- and person-dependent. The authors advise weighing these costs against possible benefit, especially when sleep, anxiety, or skill precision matters.
- The review discusses alternate caffeine sources (including gum, mouth rinses, gels, coffee, and energy drinks), but results vary by delivery form and task. Evidence for some sources is sparse, and outcomes from multi-ingredient products cannot always be attributed to caffeine alone. The dose-response and timing statements are population-level summaries, not individualized clinical guidance.
