# Summary — schoenfeld2017volume

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis examined whether weekly resistance-training set volume is related to muscle growth. Its meta-regression found a positive association between weekly sets per muscle group and hypertrophy, but the categorical evidence for a threshold of 10 or more sets per week was only a statistical trend and was sensitive to one influential study. The findings support a possible graded dose-response relationship, not a universal minimum or a known upper limit.

## Context and scope

The review included **15 experimental studies** and **34 treatment groups**. Eligible comparisons varied daily resistance-training volume while keeping other program variables equivalent; they used traditional concentric-eccentric exercise at **at least 65% 1RM**, measured morphological change, lasted at least 6 weeks, and involved human participants without relevant illness or musculoskeletal injury. Weekly set volume was operationalized per muscle group as sets per session multiplied by training sessions per week. The included participants and measurement methods varied; the analysis concerned hypertrophy outcomes, not strength outcomes.

## Relevant findings

- **Review finding—continuous weekly-set analysis:** More weekly sets per muscle group were associated with greater change in muscle size (**p = 0.002**); each additional weekly set corresponded to an estimated **0.37 percentage-point** higher gain. The authors found a stronger association using direct muscle-specific measurements (MRI or ultrasound) than indirect whole-body methods; this is an across-study association and does not show that adding a set causes the stated gain for an individual.
- **Review finding—volume categories:** The analysis grouped volume as fewer than 5, 5–9, or 10 or more weekly sets per muscle group. The trend across those categories did not reach conventional statistical significance (**p = 0.074**). In the sensitivity analysis removing the influential Radaelli study, the apparent advantage of 10 or more sets over 5–9 sets was no longer evident, and the categorical results remained nonsignificant. Likewise, the within-study higher-versus-lower comparison favored higher volume, but its effect estimate was smaller after that study was removed.
- **Authors' practical interpretation:** The authors described **at least 10 weekly sets per muscle group** as a likely target for maximizing muscle mass, while also noting that lower-volume programs (≤4 sets per muscle group per week) can produce substantial gains and may be useful when time or energy is constrained. Read the 10-set suggestion as the authors' interpretation of limited pooled evidence, not a proven minimum: categorical tests were trends, results were sensitive to one study, and individual responses may vary.
- **Limitations:** The review found little controlled research in resistance-trained people—only two included studies assessed muscle growth in trained participants—so application to that population is uncertain. Very high weekly volumes above 12 sets per muscle group were insufficiently studied to identify a plateau or upper threshold. Measurement sensitivity varied, and the authors did not determine mechanisms. The included trials and populations were heterogeneous, so the meta-regression does not establish an individual dose-response or prove causality.

## Key formulas

The review operationalized weekly set volume per muscle group as:

$$
\text{Weekly sets per muscle group} = \text{sets per session} \times \text{sessions per week}
$$

Both terms are counts; the result is sets per muscle group per week. This was the review's volume measure for the meta-regressions, distinct from total training volume sometimes expressed as sets × repetitions × load.
