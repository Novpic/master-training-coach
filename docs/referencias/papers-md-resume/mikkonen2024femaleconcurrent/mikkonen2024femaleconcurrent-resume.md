# Summary — mikkonen2024femaleconcurrent

## Central idea

This systematic review describes training adaptations reported in apparently healthy adult females who performed concurrent strength and endurance training. The included literature generally reports improvements in strength and endurance capacity, but it is too sparse and methodologically limited to establish whether concurrent training causes an interference effect in females, especially athletes, or how menstrual status and hormonal contraceptive use modify adaptation.

## Context and scope

The review included 14 peer-reviewed English-language studies of healthy, normal-weight or overweight females of reproductive age, including separately reported female groups where studies enrolled both sexes. Interventions were at least 8 weeks by the stated eligibility criteria. Seven studies combined strength training with running, four with cycling, and three with rowing or cross-country skiing. The review was descriptive rather than a meta-analysis. Searches were performed in PubMed and Medline in July 2021, with an update in PubMed in December 2022; the review was not registered, and only two databases were searched. Study quality was assessed using a modified Downs and Black checklist and was low to moderate overall (mean score 10.7 of 16). The authors note that some included reports may represent larger, overlapping research projects. **The paper contains an unresolved duration discrepancy:** the eligibility criterion is at least 8 weeks, while a later discussion describes included interventions as ranging from 7 to 24 weeks.

## Relevant findings

- **Review findings:** Across the heterogeneous studies, concurrent training generally increased measures of maximal strength and endurance capacity. Individual studies also reported benefits in body composition, blood lipids, running economy, or sport performance; these were not consistent pooled estimates and should not be read as guaranteed effects. Training histories ranged from untrained to competitive participants, limiting direct transfer between recreational and athletic groups.
- Running-based programs commonly combined 2–3 strength sessions per week with varied continuous or interval endurance work; across the review, endurance modes included running, cycling, rowing, and cross-country skiing. The authors describe strength programs ranging from muscle-endurance to heavy/maximal or explosive work. These are study characteristics, not a single recommended protocol. Study durations and designs varied, so no universal volume, intensity, or exercise order can be derived.
- **Interference:** Only a small number of studies included comparators that could help test concurrent training against strength-only or endurance-only training. The review found insufficient evidence to determine whether interference occurs in females. The available studies did not demonstrate clear disadvantages for strength or endurance adaptations, but their designs, sample sizes, and training exposures do not establish equivalence or rule out interference in higher-volume athletic programs.
- **Fast-force production:** Only five studies used jump-based assessments. Some running-plus-strength studies reported improvements in jump outcomes, but evidence was limited; one single-group cycling-plus-strength study reported no change in isometric rate of force development and had no single-mode comparator. The review therefore calls for more female-specific research rather than a firm conclusion about power adaptations.
- **Menstrual status and contraceptives:** Most studies did not adequately report or control menstrual status, hormonal contraceptive use, or hormonal profiles. One study compared naturally menstruating participants with oral-contraceptive users and found no statistically significant group difference in adaptations; limitations included multiple contraceptive formulations and incomplete hormonal verification. The review found no evidence supporting menstrual-cycle-phase-based periodization of concurrent training.
- **Authors’ cautious practical interpretation:** Evidence-based adjustments to specific exercise prescriptions for females cannot be made from this literature. For recreationally active populations, the authors suggest that the more important training mode may be performed first when both are in one session, and that separate-day training may be useful; these are cautious interpretations of a limited, heterogeneous evidence base, not universal rules. The need to distinguish recreational from athletic populations and to consider health-related factors means human review is appropriate before applying the findings to an individual.
