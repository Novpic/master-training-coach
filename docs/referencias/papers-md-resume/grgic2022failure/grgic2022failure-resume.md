# Summary — grgic2022failure

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis asked whether resistance training to momentary repetition failure produces greater strength or hypertrophy than training that stops short of failure. Across the included studies, failure was not required for either outcome; effects differed in specific subgroups, but those signals are preliminary and do not establish a universal effort prescription.

## Context and scope

The authors searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, and SPORTDiscus through January 2, 2020, and included randomized comparisons lasting at least 6 weeks in apparently healthy participants. Fifteen studies included young adults only: 394 participants contributed strength outcomes and 219 contributed hypertrophy outcomes. Programs lasted 6–14 weeks and generally trained 2–3 days per week. The review excluded blood-flow-restriction and concurrent resistance-plus-aerobic training. It pooled change effect sizes using robust-variance meta-analysis and examined training status, volume matching, body region, exercise selection, and study design. The included studies were rated moderate or good quality by the authors’ modified Downs and Black checklist; adherence was unreported in five studies.

## Relevant findings

- **Overall strength:** There was no statistically significant difference between failure and non-failure training (effect size [ES] = −0.09; 95% CI −0.22 to 0.05). The interval includes no difference, and the authors conclude that training to failure is not necessary for strength gains and does not appear detrimental in the studied conditions.
- **Strength when volume differed:** The subgroup of studies that did not equate training volume favored non-failure training (ES = −0.32; 95% CI −0.57 to −0.07). In most such studies, the non-failure group performed more sets or more total work, so this result cannot be attributed to stopping short of failure alone. In volume-equated studies, the groups did not differ significantly (ES = 0.01; 95% CI −0.12 to 0.15).
- **Overall hypertrophy:** There was no statistically significant difference between conditions (ES = 0.22; 95% CI −0.11 to 0.55). The confidence interval is wide, so the analysis does not rule out a potentially meaningful difference in either direction.
- **Resistance-trained subgroup:** Training to failure favored hypertrophy in a subgroup analysis (ES = 0.15; 95% CI 0.03 to 0.26; p = 0.039). This estimate came from only two studies, one volume-equated and one not; the authors characterize it as preliminary, especially for highly trained individuals.
- The authors could not identify how many repetitions short of failure are sufficient for optimal adaptation. Their findings therefore do not prescribe a specific repetitions-in-reserve target. They also caution against extrapolating to older adults, highly trained populations, concurrent training, or blood-flow-restriction protocols.
