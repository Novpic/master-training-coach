# Summary — jones2021sex

## Central idea

This systematic review and meta-analysis compared resistance-training adaptations in older males and females completing the same programs. Differences depended on whether change was expressed in absolute or relative terms: males gained more absolute strength and muscle size, while females had a small advantage in relative lower-body strength; relative upper-body strength and relative muscle-size changes did not differ significantly.

## Context and scope

The review included 30 prospective intervention studies (41 comparison groups; 1,410 participants: 651 males and 759 females) in healthy adults older than 50 years. Both sexes had to complete the same dynamic resistance-training program, and the review excluded diagnosed medical conditions or injuries, concurrent nutrition or other exercise interventions, and studies without sex-specific outcomes. Programs averaged 19 weeks, three sessions per week, approximately 70% 1-RM, and three sets of nine repetitions per exercise; most participants were inactive and had no resistance-training experience. Random-effects meta-analyses used Hedges’ g, with positive values favoring males and negative values favoring females. Mean study quality was moderate (14.7/29). Sarcopenia status was not reported, so the results cannot be assumed to apply to people with diagnosed sarcopenia.

## Relevant findings

- **Meta-analysis—relative changes:** Females gained slightly more lower-body strength relative to baseline (g = −0.21, 95% CI −0.33 to −0.10; p = 0.0003). There was no statistically significant sex difference in relative upper-body strength (g = −0.29, 95% CI −0.62 to 0.04; p = 0.08) or relative muscle-size change (g = 0.10, 95% CI −0.04 to 0.23; p = 0.16).
- **Meta-analysis—absolute changes:** Males had greater absolute gains in upper-body strength (g = 0.48, 95% CI 0.09–0.88; p = 0.016), lower-body strength (g = 0.33, 95% CI 0.19–0.47; p < 0.0001), and muscle size (g = 0.45, 95% CI 0.23–0.67; p < 0.0001). These standardized effects describe group differences and should not be read as raw kilograms or a difference in percentage adaptation.
- **Meta-regression and authors’ interpretation:** Within full-body-program studies, greater training intensity was associated with effects favoring males for absolute strength, while more weekly repetitions were associated with effects favoring females for lower-body strength; longer interventions showed outcome-specific associations. The authors suggested that older women might respond favorably to more weekly repetitions and older men to higher intensity, but these were exploratory meta-regression associations, not randomized comparisons of sex-tailored prescriptions. Weekly repetitions and intensity also covary, so the variables cannot be interpreted as independent universal programming rules.
- The upper-body relative-strength analysis included only seven comparison outcomes and 80 participants of each sex. The review used varied strength and muscle-size measures, and adherence or supervision was incompletely reported. Changes in absolute results may partly reflect baseline size and strength differences; the evidence does not demonstrate that one sex needs a different program to achieve meaningful adaptation.
