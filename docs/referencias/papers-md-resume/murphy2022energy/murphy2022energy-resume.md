# Summary — murphy2022energy

## Central idea

This meta-analysis found that resistance training (RT) during an energy deficit was associated with smaller lean-mass (LM) gains than RT without a deficit, while strength gains were not detectably different. The estimate that an approximately 500 kcal/day deficit may eliminate predicted LM gain is a study-level regression result, not a universal weight-loss target or guarantee of LM maintenance.

## Context and scope

The review searched PubMed and SportDiscus through June 2021. Eligible whole-body RT interventions lasted at least 3 weeks and occurred at least twice weekly, without concurrent aerobic training. Analysis A compared RT plus energy deficit (RT+ED) with RT in approximate energy balance (RT+CON) within seven randomized trials (282 participants; six studies in women and one in both sexes; mean age 60 ± 11 years). These programs lasted 8–20 weeks, with 2–3 sessions/week, 2–4 sets/exercise, and 8–20 repetitions/set. Analysis B compared 52 studies (1,213 participants; mean age 51 ± 16 years) matched across separate studies for age, sex, duration, and RT characteristics; programs lasted 3–28 weeks, with 2–4 sessions/week, 1–4 sets/exercise, and 1–16 repetitions/set. BMI could not be matched, and most participants were untrained or physically inactive. Energy deficit was estimated from fat-mass change rather than directly measured energy intake or total expenditure; the review could not calculate EA and focused on reduced energy intake in RT studies.

## Relevant findings

- In Analysis A, RT+ED showed smaller LM gains than RT+CON (effect size [ES] −0.58, p = 0.02; the abstract reports −0.57). Strength did not differ significantly between conditions (ES −0.31, p = 0.28). The small abstract/body difference in the LM estimate is unresolved within the paper and does not change its direction or reported significance.
- The separate-study comparison in Analysis B was directionally consistent: RT+ED had a small negative LM effect (ES −0.11, p = 0.03), while RT+CON had a positive effect (ES 0.20, p < 0.001). Both conditions improved strength (RT+ED ES 0.84; RT+CON ES 0.81); these are within-condition effects, not a randomized between-group comparison.
- **Regression estimate:** the no-covariate meta-regression estimated that each additional **1,000 kcal/day** of deficit reduced the LM effect by about **0.31 ES units**; a deficit near **500 kcal/day** corresponded to no predicted LM change from RT. The authors’ practical interpretation was that people seeking LM gain should avoid prolonged deficits, while the studied weight-loss context suggested deficits no greater than about 500 kcal/day when trying to preserve LM. This is a model-derived estimate, not an individualized prescription. The plotted study effects are scattered around the fitted relationship, and uncertainty widens at higher estimated deficits.
- **Limits on interpretation:** the paired primary analysis included only seven studies. Analysis B matched studies from separate literatures rather than randomized groups and could not match BMI; the RT+ED study pool had higher BMI on average. The meta-regression estimates deficit indirectly from fat-mass change, does not quantify EA, and does not establish a mechanism. Results are most relevant to the included whole-body RT studies, many involving older adults and/or overweight populations; evidence in experienced lifters was especially sparse.

## Key formulas

The paper’s unadjusted study-level meta-regression can be written as:

$$
\widehat{ES}_{LM} = 0.16 - 0.00031D
$$

`D` is the estimated energy deficit in kcal/day; `0.16` is the modelled LM effect at approximate energy balance, and `ES_{LM}` is the standardized lean-mass effect size. This describes the meta-regression only; it is not a validated individual prediction equation.
