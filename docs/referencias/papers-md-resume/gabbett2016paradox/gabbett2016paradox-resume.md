# Summary — gabbett2016paradox

## Central idea

This narrative review proposes the training-injury prevention paradox: both excessive or rapidly increased loads and insufficient preparation may be associated with injury, while appropriately progressive training can build fitness and capacity that may protect athletes. The paper advocates monitoring both the work performed and the athlete’s internal response, including recent and accumulated workload. Its proposed workload thresholds are derived largely from team-sport evidence and should not be treated as causal or universal injury rules.

## Context and scope

This is a narrative review and conceptual model, not a randomized trial or systematic review. It draws mainly on observational and applied evidence from elite rugby league and other team sports, including Australian football, rugby union, cricket, soccer, and baseball. It discusses athletes’ internal load (for example, session rating of perceived exertion [RPE] or heart rate) and external load (for example, distance, sprints, bowling deliveries, or collisions). The author stresses that the same external task may impose different internal demands according to age, training history, injury history, and capacity. Injury associations and prediction models may be specific to the population and sport in which they were developed; they do not establish that a workload change caused an injury.

## Relevant findings

- **Load associations and context:** Higher loads have been associated with more injuries in some team-sport cohorts, but very low workloads and low chronic preparation have also been associated with injury. In elite rugby league, more than 9 m of very-high-speed running (>7 m/s) per session was associated with 2.7 times the risk of non-contact soft-tissue injury compared with lower exposure. The author cautions that this small threshold reflects that sport’s demands and should not be transferred to other sports. He argues that load type and its progression matter, not merely the absolute total.
- **Preseason cohort observation:** In one rugby league cohort followed over three years, the author reports that lower preseason loads coincided with reduced injury rates while aerobic fitness improved. The author notes that this study was more than a decade old and had not been replicated, so it is suggestive rather than definitive evidence for the proposed paradox.
- **Acute-to-chronic workload ratio (ACWR):** In team-sport examples, acute load is treated as approximately one week and chronic load as a rolling average of the preceding 3–6 weeks. The author describes an ACWR around 0.8–1.3 as a lower-risk “sweet spot” and ≥1.5 as a higher-risk zone, based on evidence from cricket, Australian football, and rugby league. These are proposed monitoring heuristics, not validated universal cut points; the article explicitly advises caution when applying them to other sports.
- **Change in weekly load:** The author recommends limiting weekly increases to <10%, based partly on unpublished preseason data from professional rugby league players. In those observations, changes from 5% below to 10% above the prior week were associated with <10% injury likelihood, while increases ≥15% were associated with 21–49% likelihood. Because the data were unpublished and sport-specific, this should be read as a context-bound observation and recommendation, not proof of a universal safe increase.
- **Prediction model:** A session-RPE-based model in elite professional rugby league players used preseason and competition-phase loads and was followed by an apparent reduction in non-contact soft-tissue injury incidence after implementation. The author notes that such a regression model is best suited to its source population and may not generalize. The before/after observation does not establish that the model itself caused the reduction.
- **Practical interpretation:** The paper supports individualized, appropriately graded training and concurrent monitoring of internal and external workload, rather than simply reducing training to avoid all exposure. It does not provide individualized injury-prevention advice, and the proposed thresholds require sport-specific and human review, particularly for rehabilitation or return-to-play decisions.

## Key formulas

Session training load is calculated as:

$$
\text{Session load} = \text{session RPE} \times \text{session duration}
$$

Session RPE is rated on a 1–10 scale; duration is in minutes, giving a load expressed as RPE units × minutes (also called arbitrary units or “exertional minutes” in the paper).

The acute-to-chronic workload ratio is:

$$
\text{ACWR} = \frac{\text{acute training load}}{\text{chronic training load}}
$$

In the paper’s team-sport framing, acute load is typically the current week’s load and chronic load is the rolling average of the previous 3–6 weeks. The ratio compares recent workload with the workload the athlete has recently been exposed to; its interpretation is context-dependent.
