# Summary — mountjoy2018reds

## Central idea

The IOC's 2018 consensus update summarizes evidence on relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S), in which low energy availability (LEA) can impair physiological function and affect health and performance. Psychological issues may precede or accompany RED-S. The statement broadens the earlier Female Athlete Triad framing to include male and para-athletes, while emphasizing that the evidence and practical assessment methods remain uneven and incomplete.

## Context and scope

This is a consensus and narrative evidence update, not a new trial or a systematic review with one study population. It discusses research in female and male athletes, with additional attention to para-athletes and diverse racial and ethnic groups. Evidence in men and para-athletes is especially limited. Conceptual health and performance models summarize possible outcomes; associations do not establish that LEA caused each outcome.

## Relevant findings

- The statement cites controlled laboratory research in women in which energy availability (EA) of about **45 kcal/kg FFM/day** was typically associated with healthy physiological function. EA **below 30 kcal/kg FFM/day** had historically been used as an LEA threshold because several systems were perturbed in that setting. The authors caution that 30 is not a universal diagnostic cutoff and does not predict amenorrhoea in all women; responses vary across body systems, body sizes, and pubertal stages.
- EA measurement is burdensome and insufficiently standardized. Self-reported intake, exercise expenditure, fat-free mass (FFM), timing, and other dietary factors can reduce validity; the paper does not endorse EA calculation as a universal stand-alone diagnostic test.
- The statement summarizes evidence associating LEA with endocrine, menstrual, bone, metabolic, blood, gastrointestinal, immune, psychological, and performance concerns. Direct sport-performance evidence was sparse. One cited study reported a **10% decline in 400 m swimming velocity after 12 weeks** in young elite swimmers with ovarian suppression secondary to energy deficiency, compared with an **8% improvement** in eumenorrhoeic teammates. The statement calls for more robust studies; this single comparison does not establish a universal performance effect.
- Men in endurance and weight-category sports are among the populations discussed as being at risk, but the LEA threshold and duration associated with RED-S in men are unknown. Para-athletes require individualized interpretation because disability can alter energy needs, menstrual function, skeletal loading, and bone-health measures.
- **Author recommendations:** address energy deficits through individualized nutrition and/or exercise changes, taking account of energy expenditure, exercise goals, symptoms, and severity. The authors recommend early involvement of a qualified sports-nutrition professional. Screening questionnaires and the RED-S Clinical Assessment Tool may support assessment, but they require further validation and do not replace individual clinical evaluation.
- RED-S, eating disorders, bone stress injury, and return-to-sport decisions require qualified human clinical review; this consensus is not an individualized treatment plan.

## Key formulas

Energy availability is the dietary energy remaining after exercise expenditure, expressed relative to FFM:

$$
EA = \frac{EI - EEE}{FFM}
$$

`EI` is energy intake and `EEE` is the additional energy expended during exercise above daily living, both in kcal/day; `FFM` is fat-free mass in kg. EA is expressed as kcal/kg FFM/day.
