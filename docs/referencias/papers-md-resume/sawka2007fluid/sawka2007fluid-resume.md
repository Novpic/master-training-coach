# Summary — sawka2007fluid

## Central idea

This American College of Sports Medicine position stand summarizes evidence and recommendations for fluid and electrolyte replacement before, during, and after exercise. Its central message is that hydration needs vary substantially by person, activity, and environment: assess likely losses and avoid both excessive dehydration and drinking beyond losses, which can cause serious harm.

## Context and scope

This is a position stand, not a single trial. It synthesizes prior experimental and clinical evidence and labels statements using the paper's Strength of Recommendation Taxonomy (categories A, B, and C). It addresses people performing physical activity, with particular attention to sustained exercise, heat stress, and endurance events. The guidance is not a universal fluid prescription; individual sweat rates and electrolyte losses vary, and some evidence statements rely on limited evidence or consensus.

## Relevant findings

- **Position-stand recommendation—before exercise:** The goal is to begin euhydrated with normal plasma electrolyte levels. If needed, slowly consume about **5–7 mL/kg body mass at least 4 hours before exercise**. If urine is absent or remains dark/highly concentrated, the paper gives a conditional example of another **3–5 mL/kg about 2 hours before**. Sodium-containing drinks (**20–50 mEq/L**) and/or sodium-containing foods may support thirst and fluid retention. These are conditional examples in this position stand, not mandatory targets for every person.
- **Position-stand recommendation—during exercise:** Individualize intake according to measured or estimated sweat loss, duration, environment, and drinking opportunities. The goal is to avoid excessive dehydration—defined here as **more than 2% of baseline body mass lost from water deficit**—without drinking more than needed to replace sweat. For euhydrated marathon runners, the authors cite **0.4–0.8 L/h as a possible starting range**, with the higher end for faster/heavier runners in warmer conditions and the lower end for slower/lighter runners in cooler conditions; the paper explicitly cautions that this does not apply automatically to other activities, conditions, or populations.
- **Position-stand recommendation—beverage composition:** For prolonged exercise in hot weather, the paper cites general sports-beverage composition guidance of approximately **20–30 mEq/L sodium**, **2–5 mEq/L potassium**, and **5–10% carbohydrate**. Where carbohydrate delivery is a goal, the paper reports **about 30–60 g/h** as a rate shown to sustain blood glucose and exercise performance in relevant exercise settings. If one beverage is used to deliver both fluid and carbohydrate, carbohydrate concentration should not exceed **8%**, because more concentrated drinks can slow gastric emptying. The need for these components depends on the exercise task and conditions; they are not universal hydration requirements.
- **Performance and safety:** The position stand reports that dehydration exceeding **2% body mass** can impair aerobic performance, particularly in temperate-to-hot conditions, and increases physiological strain and perceived effort. It describes a smaller performance effect in cold conditions and reports that **3–5%** dehydration did not impair muscular strength or anaerobic performance in the cited evidence; these statements are context-specific, not evidence that dehydration is safe. Overdrinking relative to sweat losses is identified as the primary factor in exercise-associated hyponatremia, which can be life-threatening. Dehydration is also a risk factor for heat illness. Cramps are associated with dehydration, sodium deficits, and fatigue in some contexts, but the paper notes evidence against a simple electrolyte explanation in all cases.
- **Position-stand recommendation—after exercise:** If recovery time permits, ordinary meals and fluids can restore hydration when dietary sodium replaces losses. For rapid and complete recovery after excessive dehydration, the paper suggests about **1.5 L fluid per kilogram of body mass lost**, taken over time and with sufficient electrolytes rather than as a large bolus. The stand says intravenous fluids are generally not advantageous unless medically indicated; it notes they may be warranted in severe dehydration (over **7% body-mass loss**) or when oral intake is not possible. These are safety-sensitive statements, not self-treatment instructions. Suspected heat illness, confusion, collapse, persistent vomiting, or other serious symptoms require urgent human medical assessment; individualized advice is especially important for people with illness, medication use, or impaired fluid/electrolyte regulation.
- **Hydration assessment:** The authors recommend combining first-morning urine concentration and body-mass trends rather than relying on a single sign. They describe first-morning urine specific gravity **≤1.020** or urine osmolality **≤700 mOsmol/kg** as indicative of euhydration, while warning that urine indices can mislead during active rehydration. Several consecutive first-morning body-mass measurements can establish a personal baseline. Pre/post-exercise body mass can help estimate sweat loss for a particular task and environment, but is affected by clothing, urine, drinks, and non-sweat respiratory losses.

## Key formulas

An approximate sweat-rate estimate can be calculated from controlled pre/post-exercise body-mass measurements:

$$
\text{Estimated sweat rate (L/h)} \approx \frac{M_{\text{pre}}-M_{\text{post}}+V_{\text{drink}}-V_{\text{urine}}}{t}
$$

Here, $M_{\text{pre}}$ and $M_{\text{post}}$ are body mass before and after exercise in kg; $V_{\text{drink}}$ and $V_{\text{urine}}$ are fluid consumed and urine produced during exercise in L; and $t$ is exercise duration in hours. The estimate assumes approximately **1 kg body-mass change per 1 L fluid**, and requires correction for clothing-held sweat when possible. Ignoring respiratory water and carbon exchange can modestly overestimate sweat rate (about **5–15%**); the paper says this is generally not corrected for exercise lasting under 3 hours.
