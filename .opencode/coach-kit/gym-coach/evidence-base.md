# Gym Coach Evidence Base

This companion file is a self-contained, compact digest of the repository summaries most relevant to general gym resistance-training plans. It records study context and useful boundaries, not exhaustive critical appraisals. The underlying summary documents are under `docs/referencias/papers-md-resume/`; the repository's canonical bibliography is `docs/referencias/referencias.md`. This file is internal decision support only: user-facing plans never cite these sources, their keys, or their identifiers.

## Program design, loading, and progression

### ACSM position stand — `acsm2009progression`

American College of Sports Medicine. *Progression Models in Resistance Training for Healthy Adults*. 2009. **Position stand**, not a new trial or one pooled estimate. DOI: [10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670); PMID: [19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/).

- Supports goal-, experience-, and recovery-dependent training; gradual progression and systematic variation rather than one universal program.
- Its panel recommendations vary by training level and outcome. For example, it describes 1–3 sets per exercise as a novice starting approach and a 2–10% load increase after exceeding a target by 1–2 reps on two consecutive sessions. These are population-level recommendations, not individualized thresholds.
- The detailed position-stand sections and summary do not always present identical rest/frequency ranges; do not combine their different scopes into one exact universal prescription.
- Healthy adults are the main scope; medical or orthopedic limits call for individual professional assessment.

### Schoenfeld et al. — `schoenfeld2017load`

*Strength and hypertrophy adaptations between low- vs. high-load resistance training*. 2017. Systematic review/meta-analysis of 21 studies, at least 6 weeks, adults without relevant illness/injury; all included training sets were taken to momentary failure. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200); PMID: [28834797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/).

- Higher loads improved tested 1RM strength more on average; measured hypertrophy did not significantly differ between studied low- and high-load conditions when sets were taken to failure.
- Do not extrapolate the hypertrophy result to all submaximal/non-failure training: the included comparison required failure. Strength findings reflect some task specificity.
- Evidence in trained participants and some outcome classes was limited.

### Fisher et al. — `fisher2017load`

*High- and Low-Load Resistance Training*. 2017. Narrative/current-opinion article, not a new trial or systematic review. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-016-0602-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0602-1); PMID: [27480764](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27480764/).

- Useful caution against declaring one loading range universally optimal; task-specific 1RM practice can favor heavier loading.
- EMG and acute recruitment hypotheses do not establish chronic outcomes. The paper does not supply one complete universal set/rep/frequency prescription.

## Weekly volume and frequency

### Schoenfeld et al. — `schoenfeld2017volume`

*Dose-response relationship between weekly resistance-training volume and increases in muscle mass*. 2017. Systematic review/meta-analysis of 15 studies and 34 groups; hypertrophy outcomes, interventions at least 6 weeks. DOI: [10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197); PMID: [27433992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/).

- Across-study analysis found an association between weekly sets per muscle and muscle growth; the categorical trend for 10+ sets/week was not conventionally significant and was sensitive to one study.
- Lower volumes can still produce substantial gains. Very high volumes and trained populations were insufficiently studied to establish a plateau or upper limit.
- The 0.37 percentage-point-per-extra-set estimate is a study-level meta-regression association, not an individual forecast, causal effect, or set target.

### Ralston et al. — `ralston2017volume`

*The Effect of Weekly Set Volume on Strength Gain*. 2017. Meta-analysis of nine studies, 223 adult men, programs 8–26 weeks. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-017-0762-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0762-7).

- Some higher-volume comparisons showed small average strength advantages, with heterogeneous, limited evidence, mostly men and few strong direct comparisons.
- Not a basis for prescribing a universal volume, especially across sex, age, exercise, and training status. One figure-caption inconsistency for isolation-exercise comparisons is noted in the source summary; avoid using that comparison.

### Schoenfeld et al. — `schoenfeld2019frequency`

*How many times per week should a muscle be trained to maximize muscle hypertrophy?* 2019. Systematic review/meta-analysis of 25 randomized studies; main analysis compared volume-equated programs. DOI: [10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906); PMID: [30558493](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/).

- Higher versus lower frequency did not significantly change average hypertrophy when weekly volume was equated. Higher frequency may help distribute or accumulate work when volume differs.
- Untrained volume-equated evidence was sparse; this does not prove every frequency schedule is interchangeable for every person or muscle.

## Effort, rest, and range of motion

### Grgic et al. — `grgic2022failure`

*Effects of resistance training performed to repetition failure or non-failure*. 2022. Systematic review/meta-analysis of 15 randomized studies, mainly young adults, interventions 6–14 weeks. DOI: [10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007); PMID: [33497853](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33497853/); legal full-text record: [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC9068575).

- Overall strength and hypertrophy did not significantly differ between failure and non-failure conditions; failure is not generally required by these findings.
- The trained-hypertrophy subgroup favoring failure contained only two studies. The review did not establish a specific optimal RIR.

### Robinson et al. — `robinson2024rir`

*Exploring the Dose-Response Relationship Between Estimated Resistance Training Proximity to Failure and Strength Gain and Muscle Hypertrophy*. 2024. Exploratory meta-regressions assembled from 55 studies; RIR was estimated for most study groups. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2); PMID: [38970765](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/).

- Estimated RIR showed little association with strength gains and an across-study tendency toward greater hypertrophy when sets ended closer to failure.
- No precise optimal RIR or individual prescription was established; estimate accuracy, individual responses, and model fit limit inference. Use RIR as a coaching heuristic, not a validated exact dose.

### Schoenfeld et al. — `schoenfeld2016rest`

*Longer interset rest periods enhance muscle strength and hypertrophy*. 2016. Randomized 8-week study in 21 resistance-trained men aged 18–35; full-body training three days/week compared 1- vs 3-minute rests. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0000000000001272](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000001272); PMID: [26605807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/).

- In this small study, 3-minute rest improved squat/bench 1RM and one quadriceps thickness measure more than 1-minute rest. Greater work may partly explain differences; that mechanism was not resolved.
- The authors' roughly 2-minute practical suggestion is not a universal interval; participants were trained men and the comparison tested 1 vs. 3 minutes.

### Singer et al. — `singer2024rest`

*Give it a rest: a systematic review with Bayesian meta-analysis on the effect of inter-set rest interval duration on muscle hypertrophy*. 2024. Nine randomized studies, 5–10 weeks; mostly younger adults and often untrained. DOI: [10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789](https://doi.org/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789); full text: [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11349676/).

- Point estimates modestly tended to favor rests longer than 60 seconds for arm/thigh outcomes, but uncertainty intervals included effects in either direction.
- No appreciable overall evidence that routinely resting longer than 90 seconds improves hypertrophy. Allow enough rest to sustain the intended work rather than impose very short rests; no universal duration is established.

### Kassiano et al. — `kassiano2023rom`

*Which ROMs Lead to Rome?* 2023. Systematic review of 11 longitudinal randomized trials (297 adults, 5–15 weeks); most data were lower-limb and muscle-specific. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0000000000004415](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004415); PMID: [36662126](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36662126/).

- Full ROM or initial partial ROM at longer muscle lengths often produced more hypertrophy than final partials at shorter lengths in the studied muscles, but results varied and the evidence base was small.
- Prefer a controlled, reproducible, tolerable ROM. Do not force painful range or treat one ROM as optimal for every exercise/muscle.

## Training split and individual characteristics

### Ramos-Campo et al. — `ramoscampo2024split`

*Efficacy of Split Versus Full-Body Resistance Training on Strength and Muscle Growth*. 2024. Systematic review/meta-analysis of 14 interventions (392 adults aged 18 to <40 years), 4–12 weeks. DOI: [10.1519/JSC.0000000000004774](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000004774); PMID: [38595233](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38595233/).

- With weekly volume equated, pooled strength and muscle-growth estimates did not detect a meaningful difference between split and full-body routines.
- Short trials, few women/highly trained participants, and varying routines limit generalization; choose a split for schedule and preference, not presumed superiority.

### Refalo et al. — `refalo2025sex`

*Sex differences in absolute and relative changes in muscle size following resistance training in healthy adults*. 2025. Systematic review/Bayesian meta-analysis; 29 included study datasets, age 18–45 years, interventions 6–24 weeks. DOI: [10.7717/peerj.19042](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.19042); PMID: [40028215](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40028215/).

- Small average male advantage in absolute muscle-size change; relative changes were similar with uncertainty compatible with either direction.
- Supports not assigning different routine structures automatically by sex. Sparse trained data, diverse methods, and indirect body-composition measures limit individual inference.

### Jones et al. — `jones2021sex`

*Sex Differences in Adaptations in Muscle Strength and Size Following Resistance Training in Older Adults*. 2021. Systematic review/meta-analysis of 30 intervention studies (1,410 healthy adults >50 years), programs averaging 19 weeks. DOI: [10.1007/s40279-020-01388-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-020-01388-4); PMID: [33332016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33332016/).

- Relative upper-body strength and muscle-size changes did not significantly differ; effects depended on absolute vs. relative outcomes.
- Meta-regression suggestions for sex-tailored repetitions/intensity were exploratory, not randomized prescriptions. Findings are for older adults and should not be transferred automatically to younger users.

## Evidence boundaries for plan generation

- A study's observed protocol or the authors' interpretation is not automatically an optimal prescription.
- Do not use changes in BIA-estimated muscle, fat, water, or bone readings as a direct measure of trained muscle adaptation; hydration and method affect estimates (`ramoscampo2024split`, `refalo2025sex`).
- Circumference changes can be tracked consistently over time, but these papers do not support inferring local fat loss from training a body part. Do not promise spot reduction.
- The local paper summaries vary in population, outcome, duration, and methods. Rely internally on the directly relevant summary and explain the practical limitation in plain language rather than presenting a single pooled rule.
