# Internal hypertrophy planning knowledge

This file translates the repository's paper summaries into cautious programming decisions for the hypertrophy coach. It is internal-only: user-facing plans must not include citations, references, study names, author/year attributions, links, or bibliographies. Read the linked local summaries for nuance whenever a recommendation depends on their specific populations or limitations. The summaries are not substitutes for independent full-text appraisal.

## Program architecture and progression

- Choose a split and number of weekly sessions around the user's calendar, preferences, session length, and ability to perform quality work. Available comparisons do not show a general hypertrophy advantage for full-body versus split organization when weekly work is equated.
- Frequency primarily distributes weekly work. Higher frequency can be practical when sessions would otherwise be too long or quality falls, but do not prescribe a frequency as inherently optimal.
- Start from the person's training history and tolerability. Across-study results support a possible relationship between weekly sets and muscle growth but do not establish a per-person minimum, optimum, or maximum. Ranges such as 6–12 or 10–20 sets are heuristics, not validated dose requirements.
- Count planned direct work transparently by the exercise's named primary muscle. Do not count warm-ups as work sets by default, and do not allocate fractional secondary-muscle sets as if experimentally validated.
- Progress with a stable technique and repeatable effort: add repetitions inside a range before making a small practical load increase. Keep, reduce, or redistribute training when performance and recovery do not support additional work; do not promise linear weekly progression.

## Load, effort, and exercise execution

- A range of external loads can produce hypertrophy in the reviewed comparisons when the sets involve high effort; these comparisons largely required momentary failure. They do not prove that any load stopped far from failure is equivalent. Use loads the trainee can control and progress; do not estimate an exact 1RM from supplied repetitions.
- Training to momentary failure is not required on every set. Evidence does not determine a precise RIR threshold. RIR is a coaching estimate, especially uncertain for novices; use it as a practical cue and avoid routine failure on technically demanding compound lifts.
- Prefer a controlled, reproducible, tolerable range. Full ROM is a reasonable default; some studied muscles may benefit from training at longer lengths, but evidence is limited and not universal. Never force painful range.
- Rest is a means to preserve the intended exercise quality and repetitions. Evidence does not establish a universal rest duration; use enough time for the next set to meet technique/effort goals and let the user extend rest if performance is falling.

## Individualization and measurement

- Select the plan from goals, available equipment, known technique, current performance, and recovery—not from gender, physique, or a consumer scale score. Similar average relative muscle-size responses across sexes do not warrant sex-specific default programs.
- A current working load is useful only when exercise, units, number of reps/sets, and machine/bar/dumbbell convention are clear. If not, ask or prescribe `calibrate` rather than guessing.
- Circumferences and body-weight trends can be optional progress context when repeated under similar conditions. A single change does not establish muscle growth; measurement placement, hydration, food, and timing can influence readings.
- BIA and other consumer-scale estimates can vary with hydration and do not directly measure local skeletal muscle or establish bone, visceral-fat, water, or metabolic health. Do not use these labels to modify the exercise plan or make a diagnosis.
- Soreness and pump are not required outcomes and do not quantify hypertrophy. Use execution, performance across time, adherence, and recovery to guide adjustments.

## Concurrent activity, nutrition, and recovery

- When the user also trains endurance or sport, fit lifting around their priorities and fatigue. Average concurrent-training findings do not promise no interference for every user. If explosive-performance quality is a stated priority, separating demanding sessions can be considered as a scheduling option, not a guarantee.
- This subagent programs exercise, not nutrition. The project summaries indicate total daily protein/energy context can matter, but do not turn group ranges or model-derived energy estimates into individualized food prescriptions. Refer nutritional planning to a separate qualified workflow if requested.
- Sleep and recovery can be discussed as general monitoring context, not a diagnosis or fixed target. Persistent decline, unusual fatigue, health symptoms, or possible problematic low energy availability warrant appropriate human/clinical review; do not derive a threshold from body-composition data.
- Prevention evidence does not treat or rehabilitate existing injuries. Do not promise that strength work eliminates injury risk.
