# Sports Nutrition Coach — Internal Evidence Digest

Compact working notes transcribed from the repository's nutrition paper summaries. They are not exhaustive appraisals and do not replace the full summaries under `docs/referencias/papers-md-resume/`. Source keys below are for internal traceability only; never cite or disclose them in user-facing nutrition plans.

## Overall priorities and energy balance

- The nutrition chapter prioritizes adequate energy for training/health, sufficient daily protein, carbohydrate matched to training, dietary variety/fats/micronutrients, individualized hydration, and only then timing or supplements.
- A mix of dietary patterns can support body-composition goals when energy balance, adequate protein, training, and adherence are considered. No named diet is universally superior. Do not infer energy balance or assign calorie intake from a BIA estimate or a predictive equation.
- Energy deficit may reduce lean-mass gains during resistance training. The roughly 500 kcal/day value reported in one meta-regression is an uncertain study-level model result in populations unlike many experienced recreational athletes; it is not an individual calorie target.
- Body-composition methods have error and are influenced by hydration, glycogen, assumptions, and operator/device. Use optional measurements only as user-requested, consistently collected trend context; do not interpret scale status labels as diagnoses.
- One older joint position statement's stated period of effect ended in 2012. Treat any unique numerical guidance from it as historical context, not current standalone standards; use the more context-specific repository summaries where available.
- Relevant internal summary keys: `aragon2017diets`, `murphy2022energy`, `rodriguez2009nutrition`.

## Protein

- The society position summarized for exercising people gives 1.4–2.0 g/kg body mass/day as a broad range. A resistance-training meta-analysis in healthy, non-energy-restricted adults estimated a breakpoint near 1.62 g/kg/day, but its segmented model was not statistically significant and the interval was wide; do not call this a threshold or optimum.
- Protein supplements added modest average benefits in the studied resistance-training trials; they do not replace resistance exercise or establish that powder is necessary when foods meet needs.
- A pattern around 20–40 g or approximately 0.25 g/kg per meal every 3–4 hours is a practical position-stand framework, not a mandatory schedule. Total daily intake matters more than an immediate post-workout window; protein before or after a session can fit the day's routine.
- Higher 2.3–3.1 g/kg values appear in different summaries with different denominators (body mass in one position stand; FFM in another) and are discussed only as possible lean-mass-retention strategies in resistance-trained people during energy restriction. Do not apply these ranges to all users or calculate them from unverified consumer-scale FFM.
- Relevant internal summary keys: `jager2017protein`, `morton2018protein`, `kerksick2017timing`.

## Carbohydrate and training timing

- Carbohydrate amount and timing depend on event/session duration, intensity, weekly load, time until the next demanding session, prior intake, and gastrointestinal tolerance. Avoid a single fixed daily grams/kg prescription for all recreational users.
- In the position-stand summary, 30–60 g/hour applies to prolonged higher-intensity exercise lasting over about 60 minutes in relevant settings. Higher pre-event ranges and rapid glycogen-restoration protocols are for prolonged glycogen-depleting exercise or short recovery windows, not every workout.
- Adequate daily intake and total energy generally take precedence over precise meal timing. A normal meal/snack that is tolerated and fits the schedule is often sufficient around ordinary brief sessions; don't invent strict eating windows.
- Relevant internal summary keys: `kerksick2017timing`, `rodriguez2009nutrition`.

## Hydration

- Hydration varies substantially by person, activity, duration, climate, and sweat losses. Avoid both excessive dehydration and overdrinking; weight gain during exercise can signal intake beyond losses. Thirst-guided drinking is a key safeguard against overdrinking in the consensus summary.
- A pre-exercise amount of 5–7 mL/kg at least four hours beforehand and a 0.4–0.8 L/hour example for marathon runners are conditional position-stand examples, not prescriptions for all sessions/people. Do not transplant race-specific values to routine workouts.
- A sweat-rate estimate from pre/post body mass, fluid consumed, urine, and duration may help compare the same task in similar conditions, but contains measurement error and should not become a rigid fluid prescription.
- Sodium/electrolyte needs depend on prolonged exercise, heat, losses, and context; sodium does not neutralize the danger of excess fluid. Suspected symptomatic hyponatremia/heat illness is urgent medical care.
- Relevant internal summary keys: `sawka2007fluid`, `hewbutler2015hyponatremia`.

## Supplements

- Assess whether a real nutritional need exists first; food and a complete nutrition plan come before products. Evidence and risk depend on ingredient, scenario, quality control, medication/health status, and anti-doping context. Third-party testing reduces but does not eliminate contamination risk.
- Creatine monohydrate has evidence for repeated high-intensity efforts and training adaptation; evidence is less uniform for endurance-only performance. Reviewed adult strategies include daily maintenance with or without a loading phase, but do not volunteer individualized dosing; disease, pregnancy, medication, or uncertainty requires qualified review.
- Caffeine may improve performance in some settings but response varies and side effects include sleep disruption, anxiety, palpitations, or jitters. Do not encourage use when sleep or health trade-offs dominate. High doses are not necessary to discuss as a default.
- Do not recommend “fat burners,” proprietary blends, indiscriminate vitamins/minerals, or products as a substitute for food/medical evaluation.
- Relevant internal summary keys: `kreider2017creatine`, `guest2021caffeine`, `maughan2018supplements`.

## Low energy availability and safety

- The current IOC consensus summary describes a continuum and individualized, multi-factor clinical assessment; it rejects a single energy-availability cutoff for diagnosis. The familiar 30 kcal/kg FFM/day value arose from limited short-term female laboratory work and must not be used as a universal threshold. Male evidence and measurements remain limited.
- Symptoms can have different causes. Do not diagnose from one symptom, one body-composition value, a calculated energy-availability value, or a checklist. Concerning symptoms warrant a qualified clinician/sports dietitian assessment; do not try to correct suspected REDs with a supplement.
- Relevant internal summary keys: `mountjoy2023reds` and the historical `mountjoy2018reds`.
